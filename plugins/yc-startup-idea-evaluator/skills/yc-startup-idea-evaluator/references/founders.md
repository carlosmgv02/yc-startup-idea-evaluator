# Founders and teams

## How to use this file

- Use it to grade the people: traits, founder-market fit, team composition, cofounder relationship, solo founders, commitment, and how YC partners read applications and interviews.
- In YC doctrine the team usually weighs more than the idea (section 1). Grade both; do not let a strong team hide a broken idea or the reverse.
- Each trait has a probe question and what good and bad answers look like. Ask for one specific story, not adjectives: "A single, specific example would be much more convincing." — YC How to Apply [S17]
- Evidence tags: **[doctrine]** YC partner or PG general rule; **[data]** YC-internal statistic as stated by the speaker; **[anecdote]** one founder's story; **[opinion]** a guest's view.
- Idea origin and problem quality: `idea_sources_and_quality.md`. Founder mistakes that kill companies and pivot judgment: `tarpits_and_kill_signals.md`. Talking to users: `validation_and_pmf.md`. Founder-led sales: `gtm_and_pricing.md`. Small AI-leveraged teams: `ai_era.md`.

## 1. Founders over idea

- "I care more about the founders than the idea, because most of the startups we fund will change their idea significantly." — YC How to Apply [S17]
- "In a startup you can change your idea easily, but changing your cofounders is hard." — Paul Graham [S02] "The success of a startup is almost always a function of its founders." [S02]
- About 70% of the idea is new after three months in the average YC startup (PG's estimate). [S09]
- YC funded Airbnb on the founders while thinking the idea "too crazy". [S07] Brex applied with cardboard VR sunglasses: "if the founding team is strong enough it just doesn't matter". [S75]
- "At the end of the day I think YC funds people." — YC partner panel [S75]
- Graham 2026: the next giant company comes from "the next trillion dollar founders". [S62]
- Teams with no idea that incorporated first "all failed" in Altman's experiment, so "no idea" is not a pass. [S01]
- A great idea is itself read as evidence the founders are smart. [S17]

## 2. Traits that matter, with probes

### 2.1 Intelligence is a threshold, not the differentiator

- "As long as you're over a certain threshold of intelligence, what matters most is determination." — Paul Graham [S07]
- Smartness is easy to assess; toughness and ambition are the hard part to predict. [S05]
- Initial swagger or doubt does not predict outcomes; do not over-index on pitch demeanor. [S05]

### 2.2 Determination plus flexibility

- Determination is "probably the single best predictor of success". — Paul Graham [S09]
- **Probe**: "What has been the biggest obstacle you hit and what did you do next?" Bad: gave up, or no obstacle story. [S07]
- Founders must not be "the sort of person who gets demoralized easily". [S07]
- Flexibility pairs with it: "modify your dreams on the fly". [S07] Determination without flexibility ("don't give up on your dreams") is a red flag. [S07]
- **Probe**: "When did you last change your plan mid-way?" [S07]
- Altman's list: "unstoppability, determination, formidability, and resourcefulness" beat experience. [S01] Rigid on mission, flexible on everything else. [S01]
- Survival test: can they handle "the worst day and the best day"? — Blake Scholl [S40] [opinion]

### 2.3 Formidability, ambition and vision

- Formidable = "someone who gets what they want in any situation". — Paul Graham [S62] **Probe**: "When have you gotten something you wanted against the odds?"
- Ambition drives founders through obstacles "too fearsome for mere" talent; it is mostly inborn and can be hidden by training in obedience. [S62]
- Day-to-day motivation is fear of disaster and looking like a fool, not a billionaire target. [S62]
- Wang: in the AI era the scarce resource is "vision and ambition", a concrete picture of the world in 5-10 years. [S39] [opinion]
- Peer group raises ambition. [S59, S50]
- Red flag: applying for prestige or a credential; a startup is "a really really unsuitable credential". [S62]

### 2.4 Imagination and naughtiness

- Look for the ability to produce surprising ideas "with just the right level of craziness", not fast solving of predefined problems. [S07]
- **Probe (YC application staple)**: "Tell us about a time you hacked a non-computer system to your advantage." [S07, S17]
- Good: they "delight in breaking rules, but not rules that matter". [S07] Bad: an obedient, conventional answer. [S17]
- PG flags naughtiness as possibly redundant with imagination. [S07]

### 2.5 Adulthood and coachability

- Adult response to "that's a stupid idea": "Really? Why do you think so?" — Paul Graham [S09] Bad: submit or rebel.
- "Some founders listen more than others, and this tends to be a predictor of success." [S05] (Airbnb founders listened intently.)
- Altman: hard-to-talk-to founders "are almost always bad"; responsiveness indicates decisiveness. [S01]
- But do not delegate judgment: "you must always make decisions that make sense to you no matter how much momentum or inertia alternatives seem to have." — Max Hodak [S58] [opinion]
- Independent-mindedness: needing someone to say what to do is a red flag. [S09]

### 2.6 Speed, discipline and learning rate

- Shipping pace is the best predictor of success and AI has not changed that; many batch companies "are not shipping fast enough". — Paul Graham [S62]
- "I have never, not once, seen a slow-moving founder be really successful." — Sam Altman [S01]
- "For every ounce of discipline you have you can probably lose five ten ounces of intelligence and still outperform." — Caldwell/Seibel [S27]
- Judge the improvement rate: founders "start out pretty shitty at everything". — Alexandr Wang [S39]
- In the agent era the limit is "the pace with which you can learn", not the ability to build. — Punit [S54] [opinion]
- Best founders bring a new problem every two weeks "because they've overwhelmed the last bottleneck". — YC partner panel [S75]
- **Probe**: "What did you ship in the last month, and what did you learn?"

### 2.7 Builders: evidence they make things

- "The main thing we care about is whether you're good at making things." — Paul Graham [S09]
- Own-projects test: do they start projects unprompted? [S09] A project is "two people build something that was not assigned to them and get someone to use it". — Punit [S54]
- Magnitude over type: "It's not the type of achievement that matters so much as the magnitude." [S17]
- Winning "legible competitive games" is strong evidence of exceptional ability. — Max Hodak [S58] [opinion]
- Generic claims ("exceptionally dedicated") carry no weight. [S17]

### 2.8 Willingness to sell and talk to users

- Tech startups need at least one founder who builds and one who "is (or can become) good at sales and talking to users"; one person can be both. [S01]
- "You can't avoid doing sales by hiring someone to do it for you." — Paul Graham [S06] Founders who will not do sales or talk to strangers ("shyness and laziness") are a red flag. [S06]
- "If you want to start a startup, you have to face the fact that you can't just hack." [S13] The most successful of the first YC batch had a founder doing deals with executives. [S13]
- Do not delegate customer conversations. [S75] Founders "are a lot more comfortable building than they are talking to customers". [S75]
- Enterprise software companies "are sales companies", and sales depends mostly on effort. [S09]
- Willingness "to endure infinite schleps". [S09]

### 2.9 Caring about the problem and the users

- Startups often fail because founders lose motivation. **Probe**: "Would you still work on this in five years?" Bad: picked because it might grow fast or seems cool. — Michael Seibel [S25]
- Love the users: Justin.tv regained love for users only after refocusing on game streaming around a gamer cofounder. [S25] [anecdote]
- Collison: ask whether you want to work on it for 10 to 30 years if it succeeds. [S61]
- Do not start a company you would not want to keep. [S40]
- A consumer CEO who does not love and know the product better than anyone is not trusted. — Mark Pincus [S86] [opinion]

## 3. Founder-market fit

### 3.1 Definitions

- "Founder market fit is just are you the right team to be working on this idea." Friedman calls it the most important of his ten questions. — Jared Friedman [S34]
- "It doesn't matter if something is a good startup idea for someone else if it's not a good idea for your team." [S34]
- Hale's founder advantage: are you "one in ten of all the people in the world who can solve this problem"? A common job title is not an advantage. — Kevin Hale [S26]
- Caldwell's 0-10 scale: "a zero out of 10 is like, I'm going to do some kind of advanced AI startup and I don't know how to program." [S22]
- Uniquely qualified does not mean resume; it means "some angle on the problem that you understand that you don't think others understand". — Michael Seibel [S36]

### 3.2 Evidence that counts, strongest first

| Evidence | Example | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Founder is the user and lived the pain | Supabase founder migrated off Firebase in a prior company | [S01, S12, S51] |
| Worked or interned at the edge of the field | Salient (ex-Tesla finance ops), Spur (ex-Figma); "huge percentage" of YC billion-dollar companies trace to an internship | [S33] |
| Prior success in the domain | Brex founder had sold a fintech company in Brazil (as stated, identification unverified) | [S22] |
| Rare skill intersection | Diode: electrical plus software engineers; PlanGrid: construction expert plus developer | [S33, S34] |
| Family or friend access | Vetcove (veterinarian father); dentist-office founder shadowed his mother | [S34, S33, S23] |
| Deliberate immersion | Undercover medical biller job; police ride-alongs; Legora paid lawyers' hourly fee for lunch | [S33, S59] |
| Depth test passed | Could run the customer's business; could teach a class on the problem | [S64] |
| Encoded domain expertise beyond a model | Ploy's founder ran engineering, marketing and sales at Webflow | [S69] [anecdote] |

- **Probe**: "Why are you the right team for this? What do you know that others in this market do not?"
- **Depth probes** (John): "Could you actually run your customer's business?"; know their daily crises, top five problems, the cost of an unanswered call and what they would pay; "could you teach a class on the problem?" [S64]
- Experts steer AI: "you need to have a certain amount of expertise to know what to do with this boundless intelligence". — Bryant Cho [S69] [opinion]

### 3.3 Red flags

- Building for professionals they have never been and cannot access. [S33]
- Fleeing a domain they know for a shiny one; founders often do not know what they are experts in. [S33]
- Founder-market fit 1/10 on a hardware idea (VR headset by non-experts). [S22]
- Non-technical team on a DevTools idea. [S64]
- Domain where the founders cannot judge quality ("a Dunning-Kruger pass"). [S12]

### 3.4 Substitutes for prior expertise (contested; see disagreements)

- Curiosity plus extreme depth plus customer contact can stand in for a decade of experience. — John (YC partner) [S64]
- Legora's non-lawyer founders won legal AI by learning faster; domain expertise "may matter" in quantum or fusion. — Max Junestrand [S59] [anecdote]
- "Knowledge is changeable, passion is not." Expertise can be a liability because it is steeped in the past. — Blake Scholl [S40] [opinion]
- Expertise can be built by shipping. [S33]

## 4. Team composition

- **Builder plus seller**, possibly the same person. [S01]
- **Technical talent** is the biggest variable in YC interview selection: teams with it have "5x better odds" of an interview. Non-technical teams on an unlaunched tarpit idea have "the lowest odds". — Dalton Caldwell [S24] [data: interview odds, not company success]
- "Technical talent on the founding team is the thing you need to solve for first." — Philip Johnston (StarCloud) [S70] [anecdote; he is non-technical and recruited engineers before the idea]
- Business founders who outsource the code cannot tell good programmers apart. [S13]
- Inverse mistake: "the biggest mistake I've seen is technical co-founders thinking they need a business co-founder." — YC partner panel [S75]
- Early hires: "only hire people who are either going to write code or go out and get users". [S13]
- Small and select beats merely small; success depends on "the first ten employees... more like the first five". [S15]
- Hire for caring (cannot be taught) and slope over pedigree ("y-intercept"). [S40, S59] [anecdotes]
- Swap cofounder roles toward the current bottleneck. [S79] [anecdote]

## 5. Cofounders

### 5.1 Relationship

- Founders "must genuinely like one another, and work well together". [S07]
- "Startups do to the relationship between the founders what a dog does to a sock: if it can be pulled apart, it will be." — Paul Graham [S07]
- "Cofounder breakups are one of the leading causes of death for early startups." — Sam Altman [S01]
- About 20% of startups YC had funded (as of 2006) had a founder leave; some survived (Blogger). [S13]
- **Probe**: "How long have you known each other, how did you meet, and what happened the last time you disagreed?" [S07, S25]

| Good | Bad |
|---|---|
| Long shared history ("can practically read one another's minds"; Justin.tv founders friends since second grade) [S07] | Met recently to start the company [S01, S25] |
| Worked together before (friend, coworker, school project) [S25, S09] | Found at a networking event ("I wouldn't be too optimistic") [S09] |
| Committed before applying [S09] | Picked out of convenience or guilt [S13] |
| Regular explicit talks on performance, goals, roles [S25] | Unspoken resentment that becomes fights [S25] |
| Written values ("pen") separate from strategy ("pencil"); explicit ownership and tiebreaks [S47] [anecdote] | No rule for deciding disagreements [S47] |
| Aligned on outcome size [S50] | One wants a mission project, another wants scale [S50] |

- Trust your gut: misgivings about a cofounder, employee, investor or acquirer are signal; do not override them because the person is impressive. [S05, S13] "It's much easier to fix problems before the company is started than after." [S13]
- A cofounder should be "someone who you know can help make you happy when you're sad". [S75]
- Cofounder matching is a recognized way to add technical talent. [S24]

### 5.2 Three drama topics to raise (Seibel) [S25]

1. Performance: is my cofounder working as hard as I am?
2. Goals: are we trying to accomplish the same thing?
3. Roles: whose job is engineering, sales, product?

## 6. Solo founders

### 6.1 Classic doctrine

- "Empirically it seems to be hard to start a startup with just one founder." [S07]
- A single founder may mean "the founder couldn't talk any of his friends into starting the company with him", and there is nobody to hold the low points. [S13]
- "All investors, without exception, are more likely to fund you with a cofounder than without." — Paul Graham [S09]
- "A startup is too much for one person to bear." [S09]

### 6.2 Current data and the experienced solo founder

- Share of accepted YC companies with solo founders went from "about 5%" to "over 18, 19%". — Light Cone hosts [S72] [data]
- Trend of adding cofounders later in the company's life. [S72]
- YC partner panel: "we keep funding them and... some do well but statistically they do worse." [S75] A solo founder in an agents experiment "got sad" six weeks into the batch. [S75] [anecdote]
- Bryant Cho (Ploy): experience is the edge in the agent era; agents act as "400 to a,000 clones of myself". [S69] The clone figure is promotional and unverified.
- Managing coding agents is "not that different from managing people", which favors experienced managers. [S72]
- Historic exception: Zenefits got into YC with a single non-technical founder and an idea. [S26]

### 6.3 Evaluator rule for solo founders

Do not reject solo founders. Ask:
- Why solo, and did you try to find a cofounder?
- Who pulls you out when you are low? (support structure) [S75]
- What would a cofounder add, and when will you add one? [S72]
- Does deep domain experience and agent leverage replace the missing builder or seller? [S69, S01]
- If you are not technical, who builds? [S24]

## 7. Age and experience

- Inexperience is curable by starting: "if you're too inexperienced to start a startup, what you should do is start one." — Paul Graham [S09] Adulthood is not age (Sam Altman at 19 "had a 40 year old inside him"). [S09]
- But PG also says it is "gratuitously stupid" to start at 20 unless a side project takes off. [S05]
- Young founders make the worst made-up ideas and find the best organic ones. [S10]
- Experienced founders may need "a little bit more of that bravado and that risk appetite"; scar tissue can paralyze. — Bryant Cho [S69]
- Wang recommends working inside a company before founding to learn how companies work. [S39] [opinion]
- Pedigree without execution systems can "die on the vine". — Max Hodak [S58] [opinion]
- Credibility is not equal: a famous expert can raise on a white paper and a booked launch; a fresh graduate cannot. — Paul Graham [S62]
- Uncertain hackers often beat "gung-ho business guys"; Larry and Sergey hesitated. [S09]
- Founders whose companies failed are hireable; failure is education (Kiko to Justin.tv). [S09]

## 8. Commitment and seriousness

- Full-time: day job kept means half-hearted effort. [S13, S02]
- Leaving a job to work on it full-time is evidence of seriousness; so is applying several times with more progress each time. — Dalton Caldwell [S24]
- PG's real reasons not to start: no cofounder, a family to support ("this one is real"), not ready to commit "three or four years". [S09]
- It never gets easier; founders must know it is all-consuming. [S05]
- Committed early staff moved to hub cities as a commitment filter. [S76] [anecdote]
- Being the only founder in a city with no peers makes it hard. [S75]

## 9. How YC partners read applications and interviews

### 9.1 Reading an application

| Check | Good | Bad | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clarity | Understood quickly; "matter of fact" | Marketing-speak ("we're immune to marketing-speak") | [S17, S24, S54] |
| Reproducibility | Reader could nearly reproduce the idea | Sweeping importance paragraph, "zero content" | [S17] |
| Framing | "X, but for Y"; narrow description | Full generality that loses the reader | [S17] |
| Story | "It has characters, it has a beginning, it has an end" | Obfuscation | [S24] |
| Current state | What exists today: built, users, customers | "Might be built", "might have customers" | [S24] |
| Demand evidence | Users, customers, own use | None | [S24] |
| Founders | Backstory, how they met, who does what | Missing roles | [S24] |
| Achievements | Specific, high-magnitude, per founder | Generic traits; the startup itself listed | [S17] |
| Insight | Something the reader sees quickly | "Well-designed and easy to use" | [S17] |
| Obstacles | Named, with a toehold plan ("we think we can get a toehold initially by...") | Obstacles the partner sees that founders did not | [S17] |
| Flaws | Disclosed | Concealed (partners assume you missed them) | [S17] |
| Alternates | Listed; YC sometimes funds the alternate | None | [S17] |
| Details | Directions followed, complete, careful | Sloppy, unfinished | [S24] |

### 9.2 Honesty is binary

- Any "intentional obfuscation or misrepresentation" of revenue, traction, background or education "is disqualifying". — Dalton Caldwell [S24]
- Common case: annual revenue presented as monthly. Ask "Is this monthly or annual, and who is the named customer?" [S24]
- "Extraordinary claims on your application require extraordinary evidence." [S24]

### 9.3 The interview

- Core question for the partner: "do I believe I can have a productive working relationship with these founders". [S24]
- Good founders "understand their own numbers, they understand the risks, they have a crisp idea on what to work on next", are honest and self-aware about real challenges, and still confident. [S24]
- Bad: memorized speeches, not listening to the question, Shark Tank acting, adversarial posture, stretching the truth. [S24]
- Questions are basic and contextual: pre-launch, "when do you launch?"; regulated, "what is your regulatory strategy?"; crowded, "how are you differentiated?" [S24]
- No warm intros needed: YC "built a system that is optimized for funding complete strangers". [S24]
- "There's honestly no such thing as too early." [S24] Ideas alone can get in (Wufoo, Reddit with no code). [S26]
- Taste is intention: design choices backed by customer insight, not looks. [S54]

### 9.4 Evaluator protocol for the founder section

Ask in this order and stop to dig when an answer is vague:
1. Who are you, how do you know each other, who does what? (5.1, 9.1)
2. Who writes the code? (4)
3. What is the most impressive thing each of you has built or achieved? (2.7)
4. Why are you the right team; what do you know that others do not? (3)
5. Tell me about a time you hacked a system to your advantage. (2.4)
6. What was your biggest obstacle and what did you do next? (2.2)
7. What did you ship last month? (2.6)
8. Are you full-time? Would you still do this in five years? (8, 2.9)
9. What do your numbers say, what are the risks, what do you do next? (9.3)
10. When did you last disagree, and how did you decide? (5)

Grade each answer as specific story vs generic claim. Two or more generic answers on 3-6 is a weak team signal.

## Where sources disagree

- **Solo founders.** PG [S07, S09, S13] treats a single founder as a serious red flag; the YC panel says solo founders "statistically... do worse" [S75]. The Light Cone reports solo share rising from about 5% to 18-19% and cofounders added later [S72]; Bryant Cho argues the experienced solo founder with agents is a new norm [S69]. Hale cites a solo non-technical founder admitted with an idea (Zenefits) [S26]. Treat solo as a risk to probe, not a kill.
- **Domain expertise.** Friedman calls founder-market fit the most important question [S34] and Caldwell scores it [S22]; the Light Cone says building for professionals you have never been is very hard [S33]; Cho says expertise steers AI [S69]. John says curiosity, depth and customers can substitute [S64]; Junestrand's non-lawyers won legal AI [S59]; Scholl says passion beats knowledge and expertise can be a liability [S40]. Resolution: require either expertise or visible, fast, deep immersion with customers.
- **Which cofounder to add.** Altman wants a builder and a seller [S01]; Caldwell reports 5x interview odds for technical teams [S24]; the YC panel says technical founders wrongly think they need a business cofounder [S75]; Johnston, a non-technical founder, says solve technical talent first [S70].
- **Age.** PG encourages starting young and says inexperience is cured by starting [S09], but also says starting at 20 is usually a mistake [S05]. Cho and the Light Cone say experience is now an edge [S69, S72].
- **Listening vs own judgment.** PG says coachability predicts success [S05]; Hodak says never delegate judgment and there are no general principles [S58]; the Light Cone hosts say "we're all N equals 1" [S29].
- **Credibility.** YC says it funds strangers through a form [S24]; Graham notes famous experts can raise on far less evidence than unknown founders [S62].
- **Founder over idea.** YC doctrine [S17, S09, S75] puts founders first; Altman notes teams with no idea all failed [S01], and Friedman shows idea spaces differ sharply in hit rate [S34].
