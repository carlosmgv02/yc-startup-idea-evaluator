## One-line thesis
Large companies can have the idea (transformers at Google in 2018) and still fail to ship it because they cannot take risk; startups win by taking risks big companies cannot, choosing a narrow problem with a big vision, and matching timing to the founders' strengths.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Too-early kills go-to-market founders**: timing risk depends on founder strengths; if strength is sales and marketing, being early is fatal. Quote: "if your strength is to push a product to the market then being too early will kill you"
- **Early-tech founder profile**: a founder who excels at turning very early tech into a product can tolerate early markets. Quote: "what I like to do is take very very early tech and and think about what product how we can sell it through a product"
- **Too-early example**: selling chatbots in 2002 required explaining chatbots and live chat. Quote: "people ask me but what is a chatbot and I say it's like live chats but it's automatic"
- **Big companies cannot take risks**: they have resources and brand but not the ability to do crazy things. Quote: "one thing you have that that they don't is the ability to to take to make crazy things to take some risks"
- **Big tech missed transformers**: the theoretical idea was published, then nothing was done until a company combined risk appetite and scaling. Quote: "Google did nothing with it Nobody did anything with it No nobody realized the the power"
- **Legal and brand risk as a moat for startups**: an internal chatbot that said an inappropriate answer could never ship at a big company. Quote: "so we knew we cannot release it"
- **Narrow problem, huge vision**: do one thing for one industry but state the long-term vision. Quote: "if you choose a very narrow problem like doing one thing for one industry or for one set of customers but in this narrow tunnel uh have a very big"
- **Mistake to avoid**: a chatbot that does everything for everyone. Quote: "I wanted to solve to do a chatbot to do that does everything uh for everyone"
- **Wide ambition lacks credibility**: Quote: "If you're very ambitious in a very wide field then you're not credible"
- **Substitute weakness argument**: LLMs learn the world through text, a proxy, so world models learn directly from video, audio and sensors. Quote: "LLM is would like is like someone who has never go outside never left the the room they were born in"
- **Complement, not replace**: LLMs are strong for low-dimensional symbol sequences; the new approach targets high-dimensional noisy long-horizon problems. Quote: "We will never beat I think the LM at that"
- **Kill the hack**: for robots, a vision-language-action hack is slow and expensive; costs make the use case infeasible. Quote: "you cannot pay $100 of compute for 24hour of your robot home robot to work"
- **Expectation cost of big raises**: after raising $1.2 billion the real cost is outside expectations. Quote: "if you raise 1 billion and you do nothing for two years or or people don't see anything uh coming out of it for two years then it's very very"
- **Frugal culture from day one**: hard to change later. Quote: "spend money as if as if it's yours is very very important for the company Very hard to change later if you don't start like that"
- **Fundraising gets easier with each success**: first raise is hard regardless of amount. Quote: "the difficulty doesn't depend on the amount It depends on if is it your first time"
- **Commitment signals**: staff moved to hub cities as a filter for commitment. Quote: "it's very important at this early stage to have people who join for with a real commitment and not just because they want to try"
- **Directing researchers**: balance between direction and freedom. Quote: "finding the very very tight balance between direction enough direction with enough freedom"
- **Bottlenecks**: talent, data, compute even with money. Quote: "even with with money securing uh compute today uh is is difficult"
- **YC playbook for acquirers**: do not talk to corporate development unless you want to sell. Quote: "YC tells you never talk to anyone unless you are selling your company unless you want to sell"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Is the timing early, and are the founders strong at early-market go-to-market or at pushing a ready product? — Early timing with sales-only founders.
- What can you ship that a big company's legal or brand teams would never allow? — Nothing, so the incumbent can copy it.
- What is the narrow problem, and what is the vision you state out loud? — Broad product for everyone.
- Which part of the incumbent technology is a proxy you replace? — Only cost or speed, not capability.
- What are the outside expectations your funding creates? — Not considered.

## Red flags / kill signals
- "Does everything for everyone" product from a small team.
- Founders whose strengths do not match the market's maturity.
- Spending culture set by big checks rather than frugality.

## Green flags
- Narrow initial problem with clear long-term vision.
- Founder-technical match (a scientific lead plus a CEO who secures electricity, GPUs, data and offices).
- Repeat founder (second or third raise easier).

## Named examples
- Wit.ai (YC W14 [per the speaker "winter 14"]): early AI domain, sold to Facebook; the acquirer's email found in the spam folder.
- Nabla and AMI Labs: later companies; AMI Labs raised about 1.2 billion (dollars; the speaker says "around 1 billion euros").
- Google 2018 transformers paper unused; OpenAI scaled GPT.
- Facebook 2015: memo to hire 100 concierges; Zuckerberg proposed 10,000; they hired 200 instead of 400.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the vision of "take a safe idea": this source says to be very ambitious inside a narrow problem.
- Says world models are no longer contrarian, only a year after being contrarian.
- Pro-risk stance by someone with a $1.2 billion seed, an unusual case; the expectation-cost warning is its own counterweight.
