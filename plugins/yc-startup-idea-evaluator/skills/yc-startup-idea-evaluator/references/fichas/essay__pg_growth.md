## One-line thesis
A startup is a company designed to grow fast; growth rate is the one number that defines it and works as a compass for every decision.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Growth is the definition**: ask whether the company is designed to grow fast, not whether it is new, technological or funded. Quote: "The only essential thing is growth. Everything else we associate with startups follows from growth."
- **Two-part scalability test**: (a) lots of people want it and (b) you can reach and serve all of them. Barbershops pass (a), fail (b). Quote: "For a company to grow really big, it must (a) make something lots of people want, and (b) reach and serve all those people."
- **Constrained in (a)**: software can solve (b) but the market can still be too small. Quote: "If you write software to teach Tibetan to Hungarian speakers, you'll be able to reach most of the people who want it, but there won't be many of them."
- **Big market = customers x price, but avoid too few customers**: too few customers turns you into a consulting firm. Quote: "it's dangerous to have too few customers even if they pay a lot, or the power that individual customers have over you could turn you into a de facto consulting"
- **Efficient-market warning**: the bigger the prize, the fiercer the competition; if the idea is obvious it is taken. Quote: "If you write software to teach English to Chinese speakers, you'll face ferocious competition, precisely because that's such a larger prize."
- **Blind-spot idea**: a venture-scale idea is usually something others overlooked and that is obvious to these founders. Quote: "Usually successful startups happen because the founders are sufficiently different from other people that ideas few others can see seem obvious to them."
- **Founder-problem fit with a change in the world**: check whether a technological change makes an old bad idea newly soluble or newly big. Quote: "technology changes so rapidly that formerly bad ideas often become good without anyone noticing."
- **Know the rate**: founders must state growth as a ratio, not an absolute number. Quote: "What matters is not the absolute number of new customers, but the ratio of new customers to existing ones."
- **Constant new customers = decaying growth**: "a hundred new customers a month" is a warning. Quote: "If you're really getting a constant number of new customers every month, you're in trouble, because that means your growth rate is decreasing."
- **YC weekly benchmark**: during YC, 5-7% weekly is good, 10% exceptional, 1% means not figured out. Quote: "A good growth rate during YC is 5-7% a week. If you can hit 10% a week you're doing exceptionally well. If you can only manage 1%, it's a sign"
- **What to measure**: revenue first, active users if not charging. Quote: "The best thing to measure the growth rate of is revenue. The next best, for startups that aren't charging initially, is active users."
- **Consumer-style proxy needs a higher bar**: for products with no monetization plan the growth rate has to be higher. Quote: "the growth rate has to be higher, even though it's a proxy for revenue growth, because such companies need huge numbers of users to succeed at all."
- **Churn edge case**: fast spread with high churn stops when potential users run out. Quote: "Beware too of the edge case where something spreads rapidly but the churn is high as well, so that you have good net growth till you run through all the"
- **Pick a target and just hit it**: a target weekly rate turns the startup into an optimization problem. Quote: "pick a growth rate they think they can hit, and then just try to hit it every week."
- **Growth as evolutionary pressure**: adapt the idea to keep hitting the number; growth discovers ideas. Quote: "anything that grows consistently at 10% a week is almost certainly a better idea than you started with."
- **Growth must be honest**: no buying users above lifetime value, fake actives, or manufactured invites. Quote: "excludes trickery like buying users for more than their lifetime value, counting users as active when they're really not"
- **Compounding math**: small differences in weekly rate are big yearly. Quote: "A company that grows at 1% a week will grow 1.7x a year, whereas a company that grows at 5% a week will grow 12.6x."
- **Slow growth risk with network effects**: Quote: "If you have such an idea and don't grow fast enough, competitors will."
- **Initial idea may be a different kind**: the first market (early adopters) differs in needs from the full market. Quote: "the idea that evolves into Facebook is a site for Harvard undergrads."
- **Right people to bet on**: probability of success differs by founder. Quote: "For the right people — e.g. the young Bill Gates — the probability might be 20% or even 50%."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is your weekly growth rate, as a percentage of existing revenue or active users? — red flag: gives an absolute number ("a hundred new customers a month") or does not know
- Which of the two limits (market size, reach) does your business hit? — red flag: constrained in (a) or (b) without a path out
- What changed in the world that makes this possible now? — red flag: no change; obvious idea already taken
- Why have others not seen this? — red flag: no blind-spot explanation
- What target rate did you set and did you hit it this week? — red flag: no target, strategizing instead of acting
- How much of the growth is churn-masked or bought? — red flag: users bought above lifetime value, inactive users counted
- How many customers produce your revenue? — red flag: few large customers who can turn you into a consulting firm
- What is the narrow first market and how does it expand? — red flag: no early-adopter wedge

## Red flags / kill signals
- Constant number of new customers per month — growth rate declining
- 1% weekly growth during YC — "you haven't yet figured out what you're doing"
- Business bounded by geography or niche size (barbershop, Tibetan-for-Hungarians) — not a startup, may still be a fine business
- Metrics inflated by trickery — it "throws off your own compass"
- High churn hidden by net growth — growth stops when potential users run out
- Dependence on a few big customers — de facto consulting firm
- Sitting around strategizing — "Nine times out of ten ... just a form of procrastination"

## Green flags
- Consistent weekly growth of 5-7% or more (YC context) in revenue or active users
- Idea in a blind spot, obvious to the founders because of their expertise
- Technology change creating newly soluble or newly large problem
- Broad product for the market rather than narrowest
- Founders acting weekly against a numeric target

## Named examples
- Google vs barbershop: designed to grow vs does not scale
- Apple / Steve Wozniak: personal problem became everyone's as chips got cheaper
- Facebook: began as "a site for Harvard undergrads"
- Instagram: bought by Zuckerberg because "valuable and dangerous", driven by growth
- Paypal / eBay: Paypal responsible for 43% of eBay's sales
- David Heinemeier Hansson: advised a restaurant-like model for constrained software businesses; PG agrees most people should not start startups

## Tensions
- Ordinary businesses are legitimate; the essay says "Most people should not try to start startups" (note 2). Evaluator should not penalize a good small business, only tell founders which game they are in.
- Growth-first compass can conflict with initial vision: "follow that constraint wherever it leads rather than being influenced by some initial vision".
- Nuance: hill-climbing could cause a local maximum, but PG says in practice it does not.
- Slow-growth companies can in principle get big (19 years at 1% a week matches 4 years at 5%) but "in technology, companies that grow slowly tend not to grow as big."
- Weekly measure only fits startups that launched; "A startup building a new database will probably not do that."
