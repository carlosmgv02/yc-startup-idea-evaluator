## One-line thesis
Selling to startups is only a good strategy if you know which game you are playing (Stripe, Gusto or AWS style); it is a trap when used to dodge long enterprise sales cycles, to avoid selling, or when startup customers will churn as they grow.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Cargo-culting check**: Stripe and PagerDuty started with startups, but copying that without understanding why often fails. Quote: "when founders are kind of maybe cargo culting not understanding why these folks do this"
- **Does the customer have the problem?**: a product only useful at scale (e.g. alerting for 500+ engineers) sold to startups at $100-$400 a month is a bad signal even if three startups talk to you. Quote: "it's a problem that tiny companies don't have at all"
- **Big-company-only products**: Snowflake and Workday needed big customers from the start. Quote: "if you tried selling snowflake to a startup they you just waste your time"
- **Easier-to-sell lie**: it is not easier to sell to someone without the problem than to someone with the problem and a slow process. Quote: "the only thing worse than that would be to sell to someone who doesn't have the problem at all yet"
- **Willing to talk is not willing to buy**: availability of founders in your batch is not market evidence. Quote: "just because someone's willing to talk to you at all doesn't mean that that that's an easier sell"
- **Startups are not lower maintenance**: end users are equally demanding anywhere, but enterprises pay more to fund support. Quote: "startups can be super demanding and not want to pay which is a great combo"
- **Churn as they grow**: in some categories (HR, Heroku) all early customers churn as they scale. Quote: "just because you get someone early doesn't mean they'll stick"
- **Scales with people vs revenue**: HR and CRM tools scale with headcount and get replaced (Salesforce); payments scale with revenue and stay. Quote: "when something scales with people versus revenue"
- **Buyer changes**: the early buyer (founder/engineer) is replaced by a team (marketing, recruiting) needing a different product (Perfect Audience, Triplebyte). Quote: "the buyer is actually changing"
- **Avoiding sales fallacy**: targeting startups or self-serve just to avoid selling. Quote: "a lot of times i think when startups want to sell just to other startups it's because they are afraid to do the sales"
- **Self-serve is not a distribution strategy**: "build self-serve flow ... riches" ignores how people learn you exist. Quote: "just building a great self-service interface is not necessarily a strategy for getting customers"
- **Valid game 1, Stripe**: win customers at two people and keep them to IPO. Quote: "the product's gonna stick with them all the way through to like ipo and billions of revenue"
- **Valid game 2, Gusto**: startups as a bridge into SMB when the product is similar. Quote: "they could basically train themselves build a really great product build a really great user experience with startups"
- **Valid game 3, AWS**: startups as a path to the enterprise via engineers who move to big companies. Quote: "they were just training this whole group of people on how to use cloud software"
- **Bottom-up still ends in enterprise sales**: even AWS and Stripe built enterprise sales teams. Quote: "eventually you still have to have someone that's making the enterprise sale"
- **Know the game**: selling to startups is fine only if it fits your end goal; reject "sell to big companies by first selling to people that have nothing in common with big companies". Quote: "you have to know which game you're playing"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Does your target customer have this problem at their current size? — Only exists at 500+ employees or similar scale.
- Which game are you playing: Stripe, Gusto or AWS? — Cannot name a path from startup customers to your end market.
- What happens to the account when the customer reaches 100 or 500 people? — They churn or demand a custom build.
- Does your product scale with the customer's revenue or headcount? — Headcount: expect Salesforce-style replacement.
- Who is the buyer in two years and is it the same product? — A non-technical team arrives.
- Are you choosing startups because they are easy to reach, or because you fear sales? — Honest answer is the latter.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Enterprise-grade product sold cheap to startups because the enterprise sales cycle looks long.
- Traction counted as startups agreeing to talk.
- Strategy is "MVP self-serve flow ... riches" with no plan for how customers find it.
- Product category where early customers churn as they grow (HR, Heroku, CRM).
- Buyer changes from founder/engineer to a team needing another product.

## Green flags
- Product that grows with the customer without being rebuilt (Stripe, payments).
- Product similar for startups and SMBs (Gusto).
- Startups used as evangelists to enter large enterprises (AWS), with a plan for enterprise sales later.
- YC advantage: thousands of potential startup customers.

## Named examples
- Stripe, PagerDuty, Gusto, AWS: legitimate startup-first paths.
- Snowflake, Workday: needed large customers; startup-first would waste time.
- Heroku, HR systems, Salesforce/CRM: customers outgrow the product.
- Perfect Audience and Triplebyte (a panelist's own past startups; speaker not identified in captions): buyer changed as customers grew.

## Tensions
- The widespread YC advice to sell to other startups is nuanced: a superpower for some games, a trap for others.
- Against the idea that "startups are easier customers" (also echoed by Koomen on selling enterprise software to startups).
- Bottom-up adoption is endorsed but not a replacement for an enterprise sales team.
