## One-line thesis
Most reasons people give for not starting a startup are bogus (age, experience, intelligence, business knowledge, no idea); the real ones are no cofounder, family to support, and not being ready to commit, and founder quality matters more than the initial idea.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Determination is the top predictor**: Probe founder determination first; test whether they drive their own projects unprompted. Quote: "You need a lot of determination to succeed as a startup founder. It's probably the single best predictor of success."
- **Own-projects test**: Good founders started projects of their own rather than doing advisors' bidding. Quote: "the test is whether you're sufficiently driven to work on your own projects."
- **Adulthood, not age**: Check how they react to challenge; good: ask why. Bad: submit or rebel. Quote: "The adult response to "that's a stupid idea," is simply to look the other person in the eye and say "Really? Why do you think so?""
- **Kid flake reflex**: Founders should not use "I can't" excuses. Quote: "adults, by definition, are not allowed to flake."
- **Inexperience is curable by starting**: Mistakes in a first startup produce formidable second-time founders. Quote: "if you're too inexperienced to start a startup, what you should do is start one."
- **Tame animal risk**: A normal job can make people dependent on structure. Quote: "turning you into a tame animal who thinks he needs an office to work in and a product manager to tell him what software to write."
- **Effort over brains for most startups**: Check whether the decisive factor is effort or technical depth. Quote: "most companies do more mundane stuff where the decisive factor is effort, not brains."
- **Enterprise is a sales business**: Quote: "Enterprise software companies aren't technology companies, they're sales companies, and sales depends mostly on effort."
- **Product first, money later**: Popular products find revenue. Quote: "pretty much 100% of startups that make something popular manage to make money from it."
- **Users love you**: Business model cleverness does not save an unloved product. Quote: "if users love you, you can always make money from that somehow, and if they don't, the cleverest business model in the world won't save you."
- **Undervalued truths**: Ideas most people disagree with are the valuable ones. Quote: "The most valuable truths are the ones most people don't believe."
- **Popular but hard to monetize**: Seek ideas that would be popular yet seem hard to monetize. Quote: "you should seek out ideas that would be popular but seem hard to make money from."
- **Cofounder required**: Single founders are penalized by all investors. Quote: "All investors, without exception, are more likely to fund you with a cofounder than without."
- **Cofounder commitment**: Prefer cofounders committed before applying. Quote: "we'd rather have cofounders committed enough to sign up for something super hard."
- **Work together first**: Cofounders are not found by networking alone. Quote: "You need to work with someone to know whether you want them as a cofounder."
- **Idea matters less than founders**: Ideas change massively. Quote: "In the average Y Combinator startup, I'd guess 70% of the idea is new at the end of the first three months."
- **What have you made**: The key application signal is things built. Quote: "the main thing we care about is whether you're good at making things."
- **Idea recipe**: Narrow and genuine need beats broad and hypothetical. Quote: "A need that's narrow but genuine is a better starting point than one that's broad but hypothetical."
- **Own-need origin**: Quote: "Find something that's missing in your own life, and supply that need—no matter how specific to you it seems."
- **No limit on number of startups**: Do not use "room for more" as an objection; needs keep expanding. Quote: "people will want ever more material wealth, so there is no limit to the amount of work available for companies, and for startups in particular."
- **Acquirers buy strategic value**: Quote: "acquirers tell me privately that revenue is not what they buy startups for, but their strategic value."
- **Willingness to endure schleps**: Quote: "What makes a good startup founder so dangerous is his willingness to endure infinite schleps."
- **Failure is not stigmatized (US)**: Quote: "they'd prefer to hire someone who'd tried to start a startup and failed over someone who'd spent the same time working at a big company."
- **Independent-mindedness**: Quote: "In a good startup, you don't get told what to do very much."
- **Uncertain hackers**: The most successful founders often hesitated. Quote: "I'd guess the most successful startups are the ones started by uncertain hackers rather than gung-ho business guys."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Who is your cofounder and what have you built together? — solo, or cofounder just met.
- What have you made that you are proud of? — nothing built, only talk.
- When someone told you your idea was stupid, what did you do? — defensiveness or collapse.
- Is your plan driven by your own need or a hypothetical market? — broad hypothetical need.
- How much of your idea has changed since you started? — nothing, rigid attachment.
- What would you work on if this idea were wrong? — only this idea.
- Is your family situation compatible with 3 or 4 years of commitment? — the author says this reason is real.
- Do you need someone to tell you what to do? — dependence on structure.

## Red flags / kill signals
- No cofounder — "A startup is too much for one person to bear."
- Not ready to commit "three or four years" — "you shouldn't do it if you're not ready for commitments on that scale."
- Family to support (author's caveat) — "This one is real."
- Cleverest business model with unloved product — "won't save you".
- Need for structure — "you probably shouldn't even go to work for one."
- Cofounder search at a conference: "I wouldn't be too optimistic."

## Green flags
- Founder builds projects unprompted.
- Cofounders already working together and committed before funding.
- Founder who flinches at being called dependent ("If you'd bristle at the suggestion that you aren't, then you probably are").
- Narrow genuine need felt personally.
- Ability to react to challenge with "Really? Why do you think so?"
- Willingness to adapt: heavy idea change in three months.

## Named examples
- Kiko: crushed by Google Calendar, sold software on eBay for a quarter of a million dollars, then Justin.TV; shows failure as education.
- Sam Altman: age 19, "had a 40 year old inside him"; adulthood is not age.
- Reddit, Infogami, Loopt: first YC batch outcomes; "about half" success.
- Larry and Sergey, Jerry and Filo: successful founders who hesitated.
- Steve Wozniak: built himself a computer; narrow need can generalize.
- Mathematica: a startup that does require math skill.
- Google: acquirers buy valuable things, "Google is not stupid".

## Tensions
- Contradicts his earlier view that founders should be 23 and employed first.
- Contradicts the "have a business plan / know business" advice and VC revenue emphasis.
- Contradicts "need a great idea first": YC will accept applicants with "We have no idea."
- Nuance: advises against startups for people with families, while encouraging 22-year-olds.
- Claims about success rates ("50%", "I'd feel safe aiming at 25%") come from the first batch of eight and are flagged by the author as possibly an anomaly.
