## One-line thesis
Startups are still the same in the AI era: success is mostly the founder (formidable, ambitious, resourceful, fast at shipping), serious and ambitious ideas are now more fundable than in the "good old days", and giant companies come from the right founders rather than from a particular idea.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Founder over idea**: The next giant company comes from the founder; ideas are mutable. Quote: "it comes from the next trillion dollar founders is where it comes from and they probably have good ideas"
- **Formidable = gets what they want**: Test whether the person gets what they want in any situation; aligned incentives because investors share what the founder gets. Quote: "someone who gets what they want in any situation"
- **Ambition is the driver, mostly inborn**: Obstacles are too fearsome for mere talent. Look for something driving the founder; training in obedience can hide it but rarely creates it. Quote: "being beautiful is not enough the obstacles are too fearsome for mere beautifulness to do it"
- **Day-to-day motivation is fear of disaster**: Founders are driven by fear of looking like a fool and the immediate crisis, not by a billionaire target. Quote: "the thing that motivates them at that at any given moment is the fear of like disaster looking like a fool"
- **Credential seekers are a red flag**: Startup is a bad badge; there is no easy major and no prestige for years. Quote: "starting a startup is just about the least efficient way to do it it's brutally hard"
- **Ship fast, speed predicts success**: Pace of shipping new things is the best predictor, and AI has not changed that; many batch startups still ship too slowly. Quote: "there's still a lot of startups in this batch that are not shipping fast enough"
- **Ambitious ideas are now fundable**: YC funds serious ideas (intercontinental ballistic cargo, many approaches to curing cancer); judge ambition of the problem. Quote: "I say fund many of them because like any one working would be good"
- **Attack incumbents when their model goes obsolete**: A new Google does not come from a head-on attack but from waiting until things change so much their model is obsolete; OpenAI is the example. Quote: "you have to wait for things to change so much that their model is is obsolete and that's when you can do it"
- **Start from what you want**: The need behind the search was information, not web pages; the new product answers the underlying want. Quote: "what I really wanted from searches was not web pages i wanted information"
- **Founder-as-first-customer for startup ideas**: Someone who solved a problem for themselves (Sid's cancer) shows the idea "do for everyone what Sid did for himself". Quote: "somebody should start a startup to do for everyone what Sid did for himself"
- **Raise on milestones**: With little money, build the design or simulation, reach a milestone, then raise the next round. Quote: "you just do what you can on the money you got as long as you can get to some kind of milestone then you can convince investors to give you more"
- **Credibility is not equal**: A famous expert can raise on a white paper and a booked launch; an unknown graduate cannot. Quote: "he comes with automatic cred right it's easier for him to do that than some kid who just graduated from college"
- **Costs: tokens are the new bill**: The new cost category is GPU/token spend, while salaries used to be everything; watch burn. Quote: "now suddenly you got to pay for GPUs you know giant giant I mean tens of thousands of dollars a day in tokens"
- **Batch effects**: Peer colleagues, answers to technical questions, and early adopters in the batch ("YC GDP"). Quote: "you have some sort of product you can sell it to the startups in your batch no almost no matter what it is you can find some"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- When have you gotten something you wanted against the odds? — Cannot give a story where they forced the outcome.
- What is driving you through the next 10 years of grind? — Credential or prestige as the answer.
- How fast have you shipped in the last month? — Slow shipping despite AI tools.
- What changed in the world that makes the incumbent's model obsolete? — Plan is to attack the incumbent head-on.
- What can you reach with the money you have, and what milestone unlocks the next round? — No milestone.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Applying for prestige or a credential — "a really really unsuitable credential".
- Slow shipping in the batch — shipping pace predicts success.
- Obedient, never-pushed founders who "have never let themselves go" — ambition may exist but is hidden; stories of real change in a batch are rare.

## Green flags
- Formidable founders who get what they want.
- Ambitious, serious problems with several parallel approaches funded.
- Founders who treat a personal crisis like a startup (Sid's "founder mode" on his cancer).

## Named examples
- Sid from GitLab: dealt with his cancer like a startup; a YC startup now does this for everyone and helped him.
- OpenAI: the "new Google" predicted in the 2012 "frighteningly ambitious" essay (speaker's account).
- StarCloud: raised on a white paper and a booked launch, but by a famous expert.
- Reddit: cited as one of the less serious "good old days" ideas.
- Intercontinental ballistic cargo: an ambitious idea from recent office hours.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the idea that AI changes the fundamentals: "almost everything is exactly the same".
- Dismisses "lean startup" as undefined and says you can still start with little money, in tension with Collison's view that lean startups may be dead.
- Token prices are high now, but PG expects inference prices to fall "like 30x a year or something like that" (speaker's guess).
