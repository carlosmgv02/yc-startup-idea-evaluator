## One-line thesis
Pivoting is just changing your idea; do it early and lightly because of opportunity cost, beware the "uncanny valley" of a little traction, and pick the next idea by scoring size, founder-market fit, ease of starting and early market feedback.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Pivot = change idea**: should feel lightweight early on. Quote: "if you're not in a state where you're rapidly changing ideas or assumptions over and over and over again in quick succession, you are likely doing it wrong."
- **Opportunity cost**: Quote: "by working on something that's not working and you have evidence that it's not working, you are taking opportunity cost to not be doing something else."
- **Pivot equation (joke pseudo code)**: how well it works divided by months of full-time effort vs excitement plus confidence you can find better. Quote: "how well things are working divided by the number of months of concerted full-time effort. If that number is less than excitement to work on something else plus confidence you can find something better, you should pivot."
- **Months of no progress**: Quote: "if you've worked on something for months and months and months and months and it's not happening, like that's a pretty good signal"
- **Good pivot reasons**: hate it, not growing, not the right person, out of ideas. Quote: "the more you learn about this, the more you realize that you are just not the right person for the idea."
- **External-factor dependence**: Quote: "I'm relying on an external factor outside of my control to make my startup take off."
- **Bad pivot reason: dodging sales**: Quote: "they build a product and right when it gets to sales time they pivot and they do that over and over again."
- **Bad pivot reason: hot trend**: Quote: "you just hear there's some hot new thing and you want to pivot because you read a techrunch article because someone raised money for a hot new thing."
- **Most people pivot too late**: Quote: "on average most people take too long to pivot"
- **Politeness is not traction**: Quote: "a lot of founders get confused about politeness um and getting that confused with traction."
- **Uncanny valley of product market fit**: a little traction can trap founders. Quote: "Someone that gets into YC with an idea that's just a total fail like immediately is actually a huge advantage than someone that has like a little bit of traction."
- **Blame check**: Quote: "I'm not wrong. The world is wrong."
- **Product market fit signal**: Quote: "you have it when growth is not your biggest problem. It's other stuff."
- **Shots on goal**: Quote: "it's much easier to be lucky when you get like half a dozen shots on goal than one"
- **High quality shots**: each shot is a full iteration shipped to people. Quote: "if you do a full awesome product iteration of making something, completing it, shipping it, giving it to people"
- **Excitement**: next idea should make you more excited. Quote: "find something that you're more excited about and that makes you more optimistic about the world"
- **Boring ad tech**: Quote: "those like never work and it's because they're not inspirational and no one cares."
- **Easy to start and validate**: Quote: "find something that you can very quickly build and validate"
- **VC-fundable rule of thumb**: Quote: "Can I imagine this business generating hundreds of millions or billions in net revenue per year?"
- **VC-fundable: speed and exit**: Quote: "can I imagine the revenue growth to get to those numbers to happen in like less than 10 years or 5 years?"
- **VC-fundable: tech and margins**: Quote: "usually technology is a key component. And usually the founders build the technology"; "Software margins, you know, 80% gross margins, 70% gross margins."
- **Impossible start**: Quote: "Like cool, once we raise $100 million, then we will build a prototype."
- **You know in your heart**: Quote: "A lot of founders know secretly that their thing isn't working"
- **Whiplash**: Quote: "it's more deadly to your company to get whiplash and get sad than to work on a bad idea."
- **No hiring while pivoting**: Quote: "I would only add people to the team after you know your idea is working and you have confidence."
- **Idea quality score**: four criteria each 1 to 10, averaged: how big, founder market fit, ease of getting started, early market feedback. Quote: "four key things that I think you could use to evaluate the quality of an idea and you could give them a 1 to 10 and you average those together"
- **Founder-market fit extremes**: Quote: "A zero out of 10 is like, I'm going to do some kind of advanced AI startup and I don't know how to program."
- **Ease of starting undersold**: Quote: "find an idea that's easy to get started with um is just as important as the idea itself."
- **Market pull**: Quote: "if the market's begging for something and that you don't even think it's good and they have to convince you it's good, that's a pretty good sign."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- How many months of full-time work and what evidence of progress? — months with nothing and still "maybe it'll work".
- Is the interest you get real or politeness? Would they pay today? — "add a few more features and we'll look".
- Does anything outside your control have to happen for this to work (VR adoption, crypto)? — yes.
- Does this make you excited to wake up? — bored, chasing what raised money.
- Score the idea 1-10 on size, founder-market fit, ease of starting and early market feedback. — low on three or more; founder cannot self-assess honestly.
- Can you imagine it as a public company, with revenue growth in under 5 or 10 years? — cannot picture it, if raising VC.
- Do you know in your heart it is working? — founder hiding it from others.
- Is your idea buildable without raising $100 million first? — chicken-and-egg.
- Are you dodging sales by pivoting? — pivots right at sales time.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Little traction (a few users or one customer) used to justify persistence.
- Politeness confused with traction.
- Blaming customers or investors ("way ahead of its time").
- Reliance on external adoption.
- Chronic idea-switching, or the opposite, five years on one failing idea.
- Hiring a team while pivoting.
- Hardware or R&D-heavy idea with a founder lacking that expertise (VR headset: founder-market fit 1/10, ease 2/10).
- Pivoting because of a TechCrunch funding story.
- Raising VC for a non-VC-scale business.

## Green flags
- Founders who declare bankruptcy on a failed idea immediately.
- Idea the founders are visibly excited about.
- Founder-market fit with prior relevant success.
- Market pulling the product (open-sourced tool people beg them to support).
- Easy start (prototype in a weekend or two weeks).
- Early market answer is a quick "yes" in a short sales process.

## Named examples

- Company captioned "BS" [sic], YC17 (likely Brex, unverified): VR headset (2.5/10) to credit card for startups (founder sold earlier fintech company in Brazil), raised hundreds of millions.
- Retool: Venmo for the UK (traction trap, losing money per transaction) to internal tools builder, built in two weeks.
- Magic (W15): blood pressure coach to a prototype built in a weekend that hit #1 on Hacker News, profitable.
- Segment (S11): classroom feedback tool to open-source analytics library the market begged for.
- Company that pivoted from ad tech to "Robin Hood for India" [sic]: founders lit up.

## Tensions
- Pivot early and often, yet whiplash kills companies; "find the happy medium".
- Total failure is an advantage over a little traction.
- Working on a not-best idea you enjoy is better than a better idea you hate.
- Harder-seeming ideas can be better than boring easy ones.
- Even Retool's pivot scored only 5/10 on early market feedback: not every good pivot starts with instant pull.
- Success stories of perseverance are dismissed as lottery anecdotes.
