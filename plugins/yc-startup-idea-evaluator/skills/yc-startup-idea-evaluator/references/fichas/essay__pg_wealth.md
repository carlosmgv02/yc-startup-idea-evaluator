## One-line thesis
Startups create wealth (what people want) by combining measurement (small, select teams) with leverage (new technology solving hard problems), and users are the only real proof the wealth was created.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Wealth is what people want**: A business is valid only if it does something people want; money is just shorthand. Quote: "What most businesses really do is make wealth. They do something people want."
- **Do something people want**: The straightforward path. Quote: "You just have to do something people want."
- **Skeptical of plans built on what you like**: Founder preference is where valuation of others diverges most. Quote: "you should be especially skeptical about any plan that centers on things you like doing."
- **Outdoor portal test**: Liking a domain does not create demand. Quote: "it's easy to slide into thinking that customers want what you do."
- **Measurement and leverage**: A founder or team needs both measurable performance and decisions with big effect. Quote: "To get rich you need to get yourself in a situation with two things, measurement and leverage."
- **Danger as leverage hint**: Quote: "A good hint to the presence of leverage is the possibility of failure."
- **Small and select**: A small group of the best, not just small. Quote: "Being small is not, in itself, what makes startups kick butt, but rather that small groups can be select."
- **First ten (five) employees**: Quote: "the success or failure of a startup depends on the first ten employees. I agree. If anything, it's more like the first five."
- **Technology as leverage**: A solution multiplied across all users versus one customer at a time. Quote: "You fry eggs or cut hair one customer at a time. Whereas if you solve a technical problem that a lot of people care about, you help everyone"
- **Hard problem as moat**: Prefer hard problems; competitors cannot follow. Quote: "something that was hard for us would be impossible for our competitors."
- **Run upstairs**: At decision points pick the harder feature. Quote: "If there were two features we could add to our software, both equally valuable in proportion to their difficulty, we'd always take the harder one."
- **Barriers to entry question**: Expect the VC question and have a real answer. Quote: "how hard would this be for someone else to develop?"
- **Big company copy risk**: Without hard-to-duplicate tech, an incumbent takes the market. Quote: "they'll take away your market overnight."
- **Patents are weak**: Quote: "A big company is not afraid to be sued; it's an everyday thing for them."
- **Users as the test**: Measure by users, not by your a priori ideas of important problems. Quote: "Users are the only real proof that you've created wealth."
- **Optimization problem**: Treat the startup as optimizing users; guess-based work is premature. Quote: "Until you have some users to measure, you're optimizing based on guesses."
- **Ship v1.0 early**: Quote: "Get a version 1.0 out there as soon as you can."
- **Techno-weenie danger sign**: Quote: "the company run by techno-weenies who are obsessed with solving interesting technical problems, instead of making users happy."
- **Solve problems users care about**: Quote: "You're trying to solve problems that users care about."
- **Wholesale effect of software**: Delivery gap multiplied across all users. Quote: "in technology, you cook one thing and that's what everyone eats."
- **Payoff distribution**: Expect a skewed distribution. Quote: "If the mean is 30x, the median is probably zero."
- **Runs out of money**: Common failure of good products. Quote: "It's common for a startup to be developing a genuinely good product, take slightly too long to do it, run out of money, and have to shut down."
- **Acquirers go by user count**: Quote: "Not at all. What they go by is the number of users you have."
- **Competitor-driven acquisition urgency**: Quote: "the most powerful motivator is the prospect that one of their competitors will buy you."
- **Early-stage sale harder**: Quote: "it is easier to sell an established startup, even at a large premium, than an early-stage one."
- **Excluded wealth paths**: Quote: "This essay is about how to make money by creating wealth and getting paid for it."
- **Do not use famous winners as base rates**: Quote: "the press only write about the very richest, and these tend to be outliers."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Who uses this today, and how many? — no users, only technical progress.
- How hard would this be for someone else to develop, and why? — "it's easy but we'll be first", or only patents as defense.
- What happens when a big company with brand, capital and distribution notices you? — no answer beyond "we are faster".
- Is this a problem you care about or one users care about? — founder interest with no user pull.
- Who are the first five people and why are they the best you can get? — average hires, no select group.
- At each fork, did you pick the harder path that competitors cannot follow? — consistently the easy path.
- Can you afford the runway to the point where the product is good? — good product but running out of money.
- What is your plan if the payoff is zero (the median)? — plan assumes the mean.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Idea justified by founders' hobbies (the "outdoor portal").
- Technical interest over user happiness — "techno-weenies".
- Defense relies on patents — big companies litigate and win slowly (Farnsworth and RCA).
- No explanation why the technology is hard to duplicate.
- Long development before a version 1.0 with no user measurement.
- A safe-feeling situation — "if there is no danger there is almost certainly no leverage."
- Using Bill Gates style outliers as the planning model (luck from the DOS licensing deal).

## Green flags
- Small team of best-in-class peers who already know each other.
- Founders choose hard technical problems and the harder option at each decision.
- Real users, with user number as the optimization metric.
- Product used by all customers at once (leverage via technology).
- Work that can be measured directly via customers.

## Named examples
- Viaweb: "run upstairs" rule of thumb; a monster-productivity programmer adding "several hundred thousand dollars" of value; nearly tanked repeatedly; sold at the top of the cycle.
- Bill Gates and Microsoft, IBM DOS deal: luck and a competitor's blunder as part of outlier success.
- Philo Farnsworth and RCA: patents fail against a big company.
- McDonald's franchise and Wal-Mart (Sam Walton): non-obvious "technology" as a reproducible system.
- Florentines 1200 (cloth) and Dutch 1600 (shipbuilding): new technique as source of wealth.
- Salesmen and CEOs: measurable roles.
- Outdoor portal founder: preference-based idea.
- FreeBSD and Yahoo: wealth donated through open source.

## Tensions
- Hard work claim: "36 times" productivity is explicitly not defended as exact ("I wouldn't try to defend the actual numbers").
- Nuances "get acquired early": says it is a good idea to get bought, but early-stage buyers are scarce and conservative.
- Moat here is technology difficulty and barriers to entry, yet the same essay says users are the real test; hardness without users is not enough.
- Says "most startups tank" while the entire essay motivates starting one; risk-averse hackers preferred a sure $1 million but the structure does not offer it.
