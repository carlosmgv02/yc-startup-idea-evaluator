## One-line thesis
The forward deployed engineer model (invented at Palantir) suits markets with heterogeneous segments and no incumbent product, such as AI agents: you do unscalable product discovery inside the customer, sell outcomes, push contract size up, and generalize the gravel road into a paved product, but it is dangerous and degenerates into consulting if undisciplined.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Only do FDE if you must**: if plain scaling works, do not adopt FDE; it is a moat only when nothing else can work in your market. Quote: "if you're in a business where this is working for you, that's great. Don't do the FDU strategy."
- **Default advice is "don't"**: the FDE route risks becoming services. Quote: "my first, second, and third pieces of advice to people who are thinking about trying an FD strategy is like don't don't do this at home"
- **Services vs product test: margin trajectory**: early deployments may lose money; check that cost per value delivered falls and margins turn positive after a year or more. Quote: "your profit margins start off negative but then ultimately become positive after some period of time"
- **Earning the right to bigger problems**: after the first problem is solved, FDEs should find more valuable ones inside the customer. Quote: "you should be earning the right, as Sean would put it, to have access to more important problems at the customer site"
- **Target a CEO top-five problem**: otherwise the sponsor will not persist through getting a new piece of product built. Quote: "If you're not solving one of the top five priorities for the CEO, it's probably not going to work."
- **Pick the right problem, not the easy one**: sponsors couple levels down prefer easy tasks; building what customers ask for rather than what is valuable leads to a consulting firm. Quote: "where you build the product in the field that the customers are asking for"
- **Sell outcomes, not installs**: pricing is outcome-based and contracts are larger and more flexible than SaaS. Quote: "you're not selling the installation of software. You're selling an outcome."
- **KPI is contract size / outcome value**: the opposite of PMF-strategy cost reduction; also measure product leverage per outcome. Quote: "In the FDA strategy, you want to drive the contract size up."
- **Product leverage must rise**: the FDE is a customer of the product; the second customer should be easier than the first. Quote: "that amount of product leverage should be going up over time"
- **Generalize one level up**: product team asks what generalizable problem sits behind the customer's request; over-specializing for one customer is the classic failure. Quote: "which is always a little bit more general than the problem that the customer is coming in with"
- **Segment is not customer**: in large enterprises a customer has tens of thousands of users and many segments; FDE matters when each segment needs new work. Quote: "a segment is not the same as a customer necessarily"
- **No incumbent product = discovery from inside**: AI agents are a new category, so product discovery can only be done inside the enterprise. Quote: "With AI agents, there is no incumbent product."
- **Take the risk early**: large enterprises doubt both themselves and you; startups can take on execution risk, pay on success or on expansion. Quote: "you pay us if it works or you pay us when you know we're actually able to expand"
- **Who must say yes**: IT, on-prem and security gatekeepers are not aligned with the end user; you need top-down authority to operate. Quote: "who needs to say yes inside the organization you're deploying into in order for you to succeed"
- **Demo-driven development**: build around one demo that creates desire; if the customer wants to reach out and grab it, the pain is real. Quote: "you are creating desire in that customer for what you're doing"
- **Team profile**: echo = domain rebel with deep knowledge who sees current ways are insufficient; delta = fast prototyper, not a craftsman. Quote: "they need to be rebels"
- **Needs a 3x-10x change**: if the new software cannot make a step change, the effort is not worth it. Quote: "if you can't make some sort of, you know, 3x or 10x change within that organization"
- **Capability-adoption gap as opportunity**: capabilities race ahead, adoption lags, so filling the gap is the opportunity. Quote: "there's so much availability to fill the gap between what the capabilities can actually do and what the customers are able to adopt"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Why can't you just scale with a standard product? — You can, but chose FDE anyway.
- Which top-five problem of which executive are you solving? — A problem the sponsor finds easy but not strategic.
- Is cost per unit of delivered value falling customer by customer? — Headcount per customer is flat and margins stay negative.
- What did the second customer reuse from the first? — Nothing; every deployment is bespoke.
- How do you price: usage, seat, or outcome, and how is contract size growing? — Small flat contracts with heavy custom work.
- Who runs your FDE function and have they done it before? — Nobody with Palantir-like experience.
- Who is the IT/security gatekeeper and does a top executive back you? — No executive sponsorship.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Drifting into consulting: building what the customer asks rather than what is valuable.
- Bringing one customer's solution straight into the product (over-specialized).
- Product team that cannot think one level of abstraction up.
- Deployment needs on-prem or IT permission and no executive sponsor.
- A sponsor who is far from the CEO and picks easy tasks.
- Hiring craftsman engineers for the delta role.

## Green flags
- Margins moving from negative to positive over time on the same account.
- Land and expand into more valuable problems.
- FDEs voluntarily choosing the abstracted product over hacking one-offs ("internal market dynamic").
- FDE-heavy startups led by Palantir alumni.
- Ramping outcome-based pricing tied to successful volume (Castle: mortgage servicing voice agents; Happy Robot: logistics voice agents with DHL).

## Named examples
- Palantir: Echo (embedded analyst/account manager) and Delta (deployed engineer) teams; ontology as the generalization of per-customer objects; Shyam Sankar as inventor of the FDE strategy [sic: "Sham Sankar"].
- Castle (YC, AI voice agent for mortgage servicing) and Happy Robot (YC, AI voice agents for logistics, DHL): outcome-ramp pricing.
- Collison installation: classic YC unscalable starting tactic.
- Triplebyte [sic: "triple bite"]: a technical product that had to be rebuilt when the buyer became a non-technical recruiting team.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "do things that don't scale" only in scale: FDE is "doing things that don't scale at scale".
- Opposes the PMF playbook: there you cut work per customer and keep contract size; here you raise contract size and keep customization constant.
- Counter to "services is bad": services is the risk, but it is acceptable if margins improve with time.
- Hosts note large AI contracts make high-touch viable longer than in SaaS.
- McGrew himself says most advice should be "don't do this at home", yet FDE hiring is booming.
