## One-line thesis
YC weighs founders (the magnitude of what they have done) above the idea, and wants a matter-of-fact, specific description of the product plus evidence of real insight and awareness of obstacles.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Clear and concise or lost**: if the idea is not understood quickly it is not credited. Quote: "Probably the biggest thing people don’t understand about the process is the importance of expressing yourself clearly."
- **Matter-of-fact product description**: say what you build in plain words. Quote: "The best answers are the most matter of fact. It’s a mistake to use marketing-speak to make your idea sound more exciting."
- **Reproducibility test**: after reading the description, can the reader reproduce the idea? Quote: "One test of whether you’re explaining your idea effectively is to ask how close the reader is to reproducing it."
- **Sweeping importance paragraph is zero content**: Quote: "Again, zero content; after reading this, the reader is no closer to reproducing your project than before."
- **Narrow beats general**: Quote: "Better to start with an overly narrow description of your project than try to describe it in its full generality and lose the audience completely."
- **Describe as a variant**: "X, but for Y" is efficient. Quote: "One good trick for describing a project concisely is to explain it as a variant of something the audience already knows."
- **Founders over idea**: Quote: "I care more about the founders than the idea, because most of the startups we fund will change their idea significantly."
- **Fund the people without an idea**: Quote: "If a group of founders seemed impressive enough, I’d fund them with no idea."
- **Magnitude of achievement**: ask for something impressive each founder has built or achieved; the size counts, not the type. Quote: "It’s not the type of achievement that matters so much as the magnitude. Succeeding in a startup is, in the most literal sense, extraordinary, so we’re looking for people able"
- **Specific beats generic**: "exceptionally dedicated" carries no weight; one concrete example does. Quote: "This kind of generic claim carries no weight. A single, specific example would be much more convincing."
- **Insight, not execution claims**: "well-designed and easy to use" is not an insight. Quote: "It’s a common mistake to say the distinctive thing about your solution will be that it’s well-designed and easy to use. That is not an insight. You’re just claiming you’re"
- **Natural monopoly check**: incumbent bad software is often protected by a monopoly. Quote: "The reason a lot of big companies’ software sucks is that they have some kind of natural monopoly. Unless you have a plan for cracking it, it won’t make any"
- **Outliers welcome, if aware of obstacles**: Quote: "The best startup ideas are generally outliers that seem crazy to most people initially. But we want to see that you’re aware of the obstacles, and have at least a"
- **Hard idea plus toehold**: Quote: "We know that sounds impossible, but we think we can get a toehold initially by…"
- **Unconsidered obstacles are a bad sign**: Quote: "We shouldn’t be able to come up with objections you haven’t thought of."
- **Disclose flaws**: Quote: "it is for this reason better to disclose all the flaws in your idea than to try to conceal them."
- **Alternate ideas**: Quote: "It’s quite common for us to fund groups to work on ideas they listed as alternates."
- **System-hacker wildcard**: look for people who beat the system. Quote: "We’re looking for people who like to beat the system."
- **Cut words**: Quote: "every unnecessary word in your application subtracts from the effect of the necessary ones."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is your company going to make, in one plain sentence? — red flag: marketing-speak ("transform the relationship between individuals and information"), no way to reproduce the idea
- What is something impressive each founder has built or achieved? — red flag: generic traits; or the startup itself listed
- What will be distinctive about your solution and why won't the incumbent just do it? — red flag: "better design, easier to use" with no insight
- What obstacles do you see and how will you get a toehold? — red flag: obstacles the partner can see but the founders did not mention
- Tell us about a time you hacked a non-computer system to your advantage. — red flag: obedient, conventional answer
- What other ideas did you consider? — red flag: none (partners sometimes back the alternate)

## Red flags / kill signals
- Vague or inflated language — "We’re immune to marketing-speak; to us it’s just noise."
- Generic claims about the founders — carry no weight
- Obvious obstacles the founders do not address — suggests lack of thought
- Concealed flaws — the partner assumes the founders did not think of them
- Claiming a better UX as the differentiator
- Application unclear: most good groups lose in the "middle" that cannot be told apart

## Green flags
- Matter-of-fact description, "X for Y" framing
- Specific, high-magnitude achievements by each founder
- Awareness of obstacles plus a theory to overcome them
- Founders who are system-hackers
- A video submitted (statistically more likely to be interviewed)
- Insight about the problem that the reader can see quickly

## Named examples
- Dropbox Summer 2007 application: a successful application example
- Wikipedia/eBay analogies for "variant of something known" descriptions
- Google/search engine: used as an example of maximum difficulty, not maximum payoff (note 2)

## Tensions
- Idea quality is secondary to founder quality, yet a great idea is read as evidence the founders are smart.
- Obstacles are a plus (a plan with even "1% chance of working" can be worth backing) provided the founders know them; hiding them is penalized.
- Narrow description beats a general one even though founders fear it constrains their potential.
- A "next Google" is unlikely to be a search engine; "Maximum payoff is more likely to come from making Google irrelevant than from replacing it."
