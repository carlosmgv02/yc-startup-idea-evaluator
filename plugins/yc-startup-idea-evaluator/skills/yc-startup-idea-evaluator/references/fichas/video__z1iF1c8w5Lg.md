## One-line thesis
Great founders talk to users before building and throughout the company's life, interviewing about the user's problem and current behavior (never pitching the idea), then turning the conclusions into an MVP tested with the same users.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Talk before the product exists**: Quote: "great Founders talk to Future customers before they even have a product"
- **Users keep you honest**: Quote: "they are the only stakeholders actually paying you anything if anyone will tell you the truth it will be them"
- **Personal connection**: Airbnb founders gave hosts their personal cell numbers. Quote: "they weren't hiding behind Anonymous email they wanted to hear from the customers"
- **No-reply test**: Quote: "search in your email for do not reply at and see how many people don't want to talk to their customers"
- **Friends may be less honest**: Quote: "perhaps can be less honest in their answers to not offend you"
- **Video over surveys**: Quote: "you can learn a lot more from a five minute video interview than 500 or 5 000 sugar responses"
- **Do not introduce your idea**: Quote: "to not introduce your idea until maybe at the end of the call or maybe not at all"
- **Listen**: Quote: "your role here in this interview is to listen not to talk"
- **Open-ended follow-ups**: Quote: "what do you mean by that or can you tell me more about that or why is that important to you"
- **Six interview questions**: tell me how you do X today; hardest thing; why hard; how often; why important to your company; what do you do to solve it today. Quote: "what is the hardest thing you are doing about x"
- **Watch behavior**: Quote: "you want to deeply understand their behavior not just what they're saying but what they're doing because changing the behavior will be hard"
- **Questions not to ask**: "will you use our product", feature wishlists, yes/no, "how would a better product look", two questions at once. Quote: "their job is to tell you about the problem your job is to think about what features to solve that problem"
- **Problems good, solutions bad**: Quote: "users generally have good problems but also generally bad Solutions"
- **Ask why behind feature requests**: Gmail split view meant slow loading; Airbnb phone numbers meant lack of trust. Quote: "they didn't fully trust the platform"
- **Users say yes to every feature**: Quote: "users don't have incentive to say no to really any additional features"
- **Synthesize**: Quote: "bucket them in different buckets that kind of describes different problems and see which one is actually the thing that matters the most"
- **Do not over-intellectualize**: Quote: "you just want to start assigning MVP as fast as you can"
- **Is the problem valuable?**: Quote: "are people paying money for other Solutions in the space today"
- **Excel as competitor**: Quote: "Excel or Google spreadsheets is actually competitors to many many many hundreds of startups"
- **Dramatically better**: Quote: "you need to make the experience of your solution dramatically better"
- **Ease of selling**: Quote: "selling to Plumbers or contractors is notoriously difficult compared to selling to startups"
- **Prototype test**: give a goal, do not tell them how. Quote: "do not tell them what to do just watch them play around with it"
- **Think aloud**: Quote: "have them speak their mind while they're doing it"
- **Keep users involved**: Slack/WhatsApp group, ship quick reactions. Quote: "you react to their feedback very fast"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- How many users or customers have you interviewed and how (video, in person)? — only surveys or friends.
- What is the hardest thing for them today and what do they do to solve it? — answers phrased as the founder's solution.
- Show me how they do it now: have you watched them? — only reported behavior.
- Why do they care? Who in the company cares most? — no motivation or buyer identified.
- Do they already pay someone for this, or use Excel? — nobody pays and nothing exists, or a free spreadsheet solves it.
- Is this audience easy to sell to? — slow-to-change buyers (plumbers, contractors) with no plan.
- What feature requests did you get and what is the problem behind them? — building requested features at face value.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Founder introduces the product early in interviews.
- Questions like "will you use our product" and counting yes answers as demand.
- Focus on features instead of problems.
- Users cannot say what they do today or never act on the pain (client does not act on a PDF consultant report).
- Spreadsheet-level incumbent with no 10x improvement.
- Hard-to-sell audience.
- Founders hidden behind "do not reply" email.

## Green flags
- Founders talking directly to users every day.
- Users paying others for the same problem.
- Screen-share or in-person observation of the workflow.
- A community of early users who see prototypes first.
- Quick reaction to feedback by shipping a screen.

## Named examples
- Airbnb: Chesky living in 50 Airbnbs, first guest photo, personal cell phone numbers.
- Gmail (Paul Buchheit): users asked for inbox and email on one screen because it was too slow.
- Mock startup (carbon emissions reporting): worked example of Outreach, interview script and questions.
- Facebook / The Social Network: contrasted as a myth of ideas invented on a lazy Sunday.

## Tensions
- Do not pitch the idea in interviews, though the end of the call is allowed.
- Talk to friends first (most likely to respond) but they may soften the truth.
- Founders hearing users' solutions should translate them into problems, not ignore users.
- Customers' willingness to pay for a solution vs the problem simply being valuable are separate checks.
