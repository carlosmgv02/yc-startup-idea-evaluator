## One-line thesis
B2B founders get stuck too long in unpaid design partnerships and free pilots; progress as fast as possible toward short paid pilots and then recurring contracts with an opt-out period, always anchored on a narrow wedge and a measurable value equation.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Speed of progression**: the usual failure is being too slow, not too fast; check which stage the company is in and how fast it is moving to the next. Quote: "most founders are too slow to progress through these stages"
- **Design partnership smell**: 3-6 month, vaguely scoped, unpaid, low-engagement; the logo on the website feels like progress but is not revenue. Quote: "you're not getting any closer to real revenue with this customer"
- **Narrow wedge in days**: aim to identify a narrow burning problem and build a wedge product fast, then iterate problem/solution pairs until the customer loves it. Quote: "you'd be able to go away and build a narrow wedge product in as little as 48 hours"
- **Sell the wedge to 10 similar customers**: once one customer pays and uses it, replicate instead of building more. Quote: "try and take that wedge product and try and sell it to another 10 similar customers"
- **Do not overbuild a broad platform**: chasing feature parity with incumbents wastes resources and gets polite non-signal. Quote: "What many founders do which is a mistake is to try and overbuild a really broad platform"
- **Polite design partners lie**: they imagine one more feature instead of saying it sucks. Quote: "they just don't want to hurt your feelings by saying no this sucks"
- **Unpaid dev shop**: a growing bespoke requirements list from a free customer is a bad relationship. Quote: "The founders understandably want to make their first customer really, really happy, but they're too meek to ask for money."
- **Pilot needs a success definition**: know what you are trying to prove and the agreed metrics (value equation: saving or uplift vs price). Quote: "what are you trying to prove? What are the agreed success metrics you're aiming for?"
- **Value equation example**: claim e.g. 20% of inbound queries solved, team 100 to 80, about a million in salaries saved, charge $200,000; pilot proves the percentage on a sample (1,000 queries). Quote: "If they pay you $200,000, they're going to get a million dollars of benefit."
- **Low-risk pilot designs**: backtesting on historical data, side-by-side with human agents, 1% of volume, a smaller geography, so the champion is not exposed. Quote: "your champion is not going to get fired if it all goes wrong"
- **Willingness-to-pay conversation**: have it early; disqualify customers not ready, able or willing to buy. Quote: "you really need to disqualify customers who just aren't ready, able, or willing to buy your product"
- **Paid pilot over free**: financial commitment makes the customer take it seriously; also ask the full-product price upfront. Quote: "Getting a financial commitment from the customer is going to make them take your pilot much, much more seriously"
- **Champion's approval limit**: ask what the champion can approve personally (e.g. $10,000-$20,000 card) to skip procurement; take less money to shortcut approval. Quote: "I'd often take less money if it allows you to shortcut a long long approval process"
- **Pilot logistics**: wait for a live project, require client data and a dedicated person, check in every couple of days, 7-14 days if the product is dialed in. Quote: "I'd strongly advise you also schedule check-ins every couple of days with these people"
- **Time to first value as north star**: janky is fine (Excel or emailed data); no full API integration needing customer engineering. Quote: "I'd track the time to first value like a northstar metric, reducing it from weeks to hours"
- **Book the post-pilot meeting upfront**: show hard ROI so keep/kill is obvious. Quote: "I'd also insist you book a post-pilot meeting before the pilot even starts"
- **Recurring contract with opt-out**: monthly/annual contract with 30-60 day money-back; converts by default, no second sales process. Quote: "one sales process that turns into a recurring revenue contract"
- **Early product = selling the founders**: the promise is that you personally fix problems, including a cell number. Quote: "You're actually selling the founders and the early team."
- **Customer success after signature**: signed is not implemented. Quote: "signed $4 million worth of contracts, but they implemented less than $2 million of those contracts."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is the narrowest wedge you could ship to a customer in 48 hours? — Needs a full platform first.
- Is any customer paying today, and if not, why not? — "They said they would pay later" or "logo is worth it".
- What is the value equation and who agreed to the metric? — No quantified saving or uplift.
- What did you ask the customer to commit beyond money (data, a named team, a project date)? — Nothing; they are "too busy".
- What is your time to first value? — Weeks or needs customer engineering for integration.
- What does the contract do by default after the pilot? — Another full negotiation required.
- Who is the economic buyer, technical approver, security gatekeeper, legal, daily users? — Only one contact mapped.
- Is SOC 2 started? — Not started in an enterprise-targeted company.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Long unpaid design partnership with fancy logo and no revenue path.
- Free pilot with no target, no end date and low customer engagement.
- Founder afraid to raise price or willingness-to-pay.
- Customer gives a long, growing, bespoke requirements list.
- Counting opt-out-period contracts as ARR without telling investors.
- Signed contracts far above implemented contracts (missing customer success).
- Weeks of legal redlining before anything has been proved.

## Green flags
- Paid pilot of 7-14 days with agreed metrics and a booked end meeting.
- Internal champion who sells when you are not in the room and fights budget battles.
- Closing a recurring contract every week or two.
- Customer references available ("customer X, Y and Z all signed on these terms").
- Founders doing the work themselves or even going undercover in the domain (auditor, real estate agent, accountant).

## Named examples
- Hypothetical customer-service AI: 20% of queries, $200,000 price, as the value-equation template.
- A YC company with $4 million contracted but under $2 million implemented: shows missing customer success.
- Vanta [sic: "Vant"]: example of a SOC 2 helper YC company.
- A founder in France asked for a "pre-proof concept": an example of absurdly low commitment.

## Tensions
- Says "not all design partnerships are a waste of time, just most of them", while also valuing sitting next to customers for a few days.
- Advocates being flexible on contract terms (only fight company-ending clauses), contra the usual lawyer caution.
- Opt-out contracts are the pro move but complicate ARR reporting to investors.
- Contrasts with Koomen (free/cheap is a mistake) mostly in emphasis, not substance; both push for paid early.
- Scarcity ("capacity for two enterprise customers this quarter") is suggested as a closing tactic.
