## One-line thesis
Understanding your users is the key: the hard part of a startup is knowing what to make for them, and most other principles (launch fast, evolve the idea, great service) exist to serve that.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Cofounders are the fixed asset**: check the founding team first; the idea can change, the cofounders are hard to change. Weak or mismatched team = weak bet regardless of idea. Quote: "In a startup you can change your idea easily, but changing your cofounders is hard."
- **Success is a function of founders**: judge the people before the market. Quote: "the success of a startup is almost always a function of its founders."
- **Launch is the start of work**: has the team shipped something to real users? Good = launched and learning; bad = still building in private. Quote: "you haven't really started working on it till you've launched. Launching teaches you what you should have been building."
- **Launch as pretext**: the first version matters mostly as a way to engage users, not as the product. Quote: "the main value of whatever you launch with is as a pretext for engaging users."
- **Idea evolves**: founders who treat the idea as a finished brilliant plan are making a mistake; good founders show iteration history. Quote: "It's a big mistake to treat a startup as if it were merely a matter of implementing some brilliant initial idea."
- **Depth of improvement**: wealth = users x how much you improve their lives; the second dimension is the controllable one. Ask how much better life is for each user. Quote: "the growth in the first will be driven by how well you do in the second."
- **Seeing something users lack**: the hard part is asking the right question, which requires understanding users. Quote: "the hard part is seeing something new that users lack. The better you understand them the better the odds of doing that."
- **Founder-needed product**: founders who built what they themselves needed have better odds. Quote: "That's why so many successful startups make something the founders needed."
- **Few users love you**: prefer fully satisfying a subset of users over partly satisfying everyone. Good = a small group that loves it; bad = broad lukewarm interest. Quote: "Initially you have to choose between satisfying all the needs of a subset of potential users, or satisfying a subset of the needs of all potential users. Take the first."
- **Honest feedback loop**: with a narrow loved product it is harder to fool yourself, since user count is observable. Quote: "it's harder to lie to yourself. If you think you're 85% of the way to a great product, how do you know it's not 70%? Or 10%?"
- **Unscalable service early**: good = founders go out of their way for early customers to learn about them. Quote: "In the earliest stages of a startup, it pays to offer customer service on a level that wouldn't scale, because it's a way of learning about your users."
- **Incumbents' bad service is an opening**: customers are used to being maltreated, so surprisingly good service stands out. Quote: "Most of the companies they deal with are quasi-monopolies that get away with atrocious customer service."
- **Measure the right thing**: does the team track a single number daily (users)? Careful that the metric is the right one. Quote: "Corollary: be careful what you measure."
- **Cheapness**: the most common death is running out of money, so low burn equals more iterations. Quote: "Most startups fail before they make something people want, and the most common form of failure is running out of money."
- **Ramen profitable**: covering founders' living expenses changes the investor relationship. Quote: "Once you cross over into ramen profitable, it completely changes your relationship with investors."
- **Distractions that pay**: day jobs, consulting, profitable side projects pull focus. Quote: "The worst type are those that pay money: day jobs, consulting, profitable side-projects."
- **Persistence plus morphing**: effort works in startups if the idea keeps changing. Quote: "Sheer effort is usually enough, so long as you keep morphing your idea."
- **Do not depend on deals**: treat pending deals as background processes; do not forecast on unsigned deals. Quote: "It's very dangerous to morale to start to depend on deals closing, not just because they so often don't, but because it makes them less likely to."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Who exactly are your users, and how many love the product today? — red flag: a large number of "interested" people and no small group that loves it
- What have you learned since launching that changed the idea? — red flag: nothing changed, or not launched
- How much better is a user's life with your product? — red flag: cannot be stated concretely
- Did you need this yourself, or how do you know the problem? — red flag: no first-hand contact with users
- What do you do for your first customers that does not scale? — red flag: none, or service is outsourced
- What single number do you plot every day? — red flag: no number, or a vanity number
- How many months of runway and what is your burn? — red flag: spending ahead of finding something people want
- Are the founders full time with no paying distractions? — red flag: consulting or a day job funds the effort
- Which revenue is signed versus a deal "in progress"? — red flag: plan depends on deals closing

## Red flags / kill signals
- Cofounders poorly matched or unstable — hard to change later, success is a function of founders
- Not launched yet — "you haven't really started working on it till you've launched"
- Product that makes many people semi-happy and none delighted — satisfaction is hard to measure and hard to expand
- Burn high before product-market fit — running out of money is the usual cause of death
- Paying distractions (consulting, day job) pulling the team away
- Team demoralized and unfocused — underlying cause of death is usually lack of focus
- Morale tied to deals closing

## Green flags
- Founders who built something they needed
- Ten users who love the product
- Visible iteration since launch
- Cheap, fast iteration and near ramen profitability
- Founders who give surprisingly good, hands-on service to early users

## Named examples
- Paul Buchheit: source of "make a few people really happy" principle
- Joe Kraus: source of "you make what you measure"
- Viaweb: about 20 deals fell through; taught not to depend on deals

## Tensions
- Favor depth over breadth of users now, even though large user numbers are the eventual goal ("Ideally you want to make large numbers of users love you, but you can't expect to hit that right away").
- Doing things that do not scale (service) is advised early, against scale-first thinking.
- Fundraising is itself listed as a distraction, nuancing "raise as soon as possible".
