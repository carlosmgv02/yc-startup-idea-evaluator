## One-line thesis
The biggest startup ideas feel frightening and are invisible to most idea-seekers because the subconscious filters them out; the way to pursue them is to start with a deceptively small, working wedge and expand in a general direction, not toward a precise blueprint.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Fear as a signal**: If a huge idea makes you shrink away, that is normal and not a reason to discard it; check whether you filtered an idea out only because it threatens your identity. Quote: "The biggest startup ideas are terrifying. And not just because they'd be a lot of work. The biggest ideas seem to threaten your identity"
- **Right side of impossible**: Good ambitious ideas are hard but with visible signs they might work (e.g. cracks in an incumbent). Quote: "The best ideas are just on the right side of impossible."
- **Incumbent drifting from its core**: Look for an incumbent doing things it is not good at or has degraded on, so switching becomes thinkable. Quote: "for the first time in over a decade the idea of switching seems thinkable to me."
- **Tiny elite beachhead**: A small, powerful first user group beats broad reach. Quote: "A search engine whose users consisted of the top 10,000 hackers and no one else would be in a very powerful position despite its small size"
- **Build what you want (founder-user fit)**: If the founder is the target user, the route is straightforward. Quote: "make the search engine you yourself want."
- **Core of users before long-term constraints**: Early product decisions that limit the long term are acceptable if they win the initial core. Quote: "if you don't get that initial core of users, there won't be a long term."
- **Fast beats feature-parity**: A speed advantage alone can pull users from a slow incumbent. Quote: "If you made something no better than GMail, but fast, that alone would let you start to pull users away from GMail."
- **Willingness to pay is much higher than assumed for time sinks**: Price against the hours the problem eats. Quote: "I'd have no problem paying $50 a month. Considering how much time I spend in email"
- **Chicken-and-egg escape via powerful early adopters**: Check whether influential people suffer the same problem and would switch first. Quote: "some of the most powerful people in the world will be among the first to switch to it."
- **Do not replace wholesale**: Incumbent institutions are eroded, not replaced; target the lost monopoly on a slice. Quote: "They'll just lose the de facto monopoly on certain types of learning that they once had."
- **Secondary effects of unbundling**: Big problems unbundle credentials, social life, etc., creating more startup surface. Quote: "If learning breaks up into many little pieces, credentialling may separate from it."
- **Avoid bureaucratic-obstacle entry points**: Pick the segment with fewer obstacles first. Quote: "You could replace high schools too, but there you face bureaucratic obstacles that would slow down a startup. Universities seem the place to start."
- **Infrastructure fallback**: If platforms overreach, a la carte infrastructure for producers becomes a business. Quote: "If that's the way things play out, there will also be a need for such infrastructure companies."
- **Visionary CEO comes from founding**: Product visionaries cannot be hired. Quote: "Empirically the way you get a product visionary as CEO is for him to found the company and not get fired."
- **Revenue is a lagging indicator**: Do not read incumbent revenue as proof of product vitality. Quote: "revenue is a lagging indicator in the technology business."
- **High expected value despite low odds**: A low-probability, enormous-upside idea can still be worth trying. Quote: "The expected value would be high even if the chance of succeeding was low."
- **Distribution via web services**: A technical advantage delivered as a service captures practically all users. Quote: "you could offer it to them as a web service. And that would in turn mean that you got practically all the users."
- **Human-in-the-loop middle path**: When full automation is infeasible, ship something that looks automatic with people inside. Quote: "something that looks to the user like the sufficiently smart compiler, but inside has people"
- **Idea generation by future embarrassment**: Imagine what will seem backward to future generations. Quote: "imagine the ways in which we'll seem backward to future generations."
- **Don't announce the big goal**: Framing a startup as replacing something huge raises expectations and creates haters. Quote: "Just say you're building todo-list software. That sounds harmless."
- **Start small, expand westward**: Begin with something that works and expand in a general direction. Quote: "Start with something you know works, and when you expand, expand westward."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Who are the first 10,000 (or fewer) users who would prefer this to the incumbent, and why? — "everyone" or no specific beachhead group.
- What is the deceptively small thing you ship first that already works? — only a grand vision with no working wedge.
- Where is the incumbent weak or distracted right now? — "they are huge" with no identified crack.
- Would you yourself use this daily? — the founder is not the user.
- How much would a power user pay, measured against time spent on the problem? — no price anchor.
- Which obstacles (bureaucratic, entrenched protocol, tradition) sit in your path, and which segment avoids them? — starting in the most regulated or bureaucratic segment.
- Is your plan a precise future you intend to reach, or a direction you will correct? — rigid blueprint.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Pitching a frontal attack on a giant incumbent or institution — "you raise too many expectations" and create "an army of haters".
- A precise long-range blueprint — "your current blueprint is almost certainly mistaken."
- Ambition so big the start is far from a working product — "the bigger your ambition, the longer it's going to take, and the further you project into the future, the more likely you'll get it wrong."
- Entering through high-bureaucracy segments first (high schools versus universities).

## Green flags
- Idea that scares people off, so it looks invisible to competitors.
- Founder is the ideal first user (the hacker search engine).
- Elite or influential early adopters who share the pain and can switch fast.
- A cheap, working initial product in a niche (Basic interpreter, Harvard site).
- Incumbent visibly degrading or drifting (Google search clutter, GMail slow).
- Objections come from entrenched conventional wisdom (e.g. "the forum troll ... doesn't even know where to begin").

## Named examples
- Google, Microsoft: an incumbent that is set an agenda by a rival and drifts from its strengths (Microsoft into search; Google away from its clean search).
- Facebook, Harvard undergrads: a tiny core of users as "10% of the way to an IPO".
- Apple, Steve Jobs: visionary CEOs come from founders; Apple as proof of possibility.
- Roger Bannister: showing it can be done lowers the perceived impossibility for successors.
- Bill Gates, Mark Zuckerberg: neither knew how big their companies would get.
- Hadoop and MapReduce: the least ambitious route to parallelism.
- Bill Clinton 2004 bypass: illustrates waiting for symptoms versus continuous diagnosis.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the VC habit of judging a founder by a clear plan: "in the biggest successes there is the least correlation between the initial plan and what the startup eventually becomes."
- Praises huge ambition yet says "Maybe it's a bad idea to have really big ambitions initially."
- Nuances "build something people want": the ideas look bad or crazy to others by design; objections from conventional wisdom are a positive signal.
- "Don't announce the big vision" nuances fundraising narratives that lead with the grand vision.
