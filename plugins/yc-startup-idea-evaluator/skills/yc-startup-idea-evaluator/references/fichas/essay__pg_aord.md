## One-line thesis
For a startup older than 8 or 9 months, the first question is whether, at current expenses and recent growth, it reaches profitability on the money left; if not, it must fix the product or growth, not hire or hope for investors.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Default alive test**: with constant expenses and recent revenue growth, do they reach profitability on remaining cash? Alive means talk ambition, dead means talk rescue. Quote: "Assuming their expenses remain constant and their revenue growth is what it has been over the last several months, do they make it to profitability on the money they have left?"
- **Founder self-knowledge**: a founder who cannot answer the question is itself a signal. Quote: "Half the founders I talk to don't know whether they're default alive or default dead."
- **Fatal pinch**: check the three-way combination. Quote: "The fatal pinch is default dead + slow growth + not enough time to fix it."
- **Separate facts from hopes**: make founders say the hope out loud. Quote: "We're default dead, but we're counting on investors to save us."
- **Growth threshold for investor interest**: steep revenue growth makes investor interest a reasonable bet. Quote: "If you have steep revenue growth, say over 5x a year, you can start to count on investors being interested even if you're not profitable."
- **Plan B written down**: fundraising is only a plan A; need a written survival plan and a switch date. Quote: "you should know (as in write down) precisely what you'll need to do to survive if you can't raise more money, and precisely when you'll have to switch to plan B if plan A isn't working."
- **Spend vs growth are weakly linked**: fast growth comes from hitting a need, not from spending. Quote: "there is surprisingly little connection between how much a startup spends and how fast it grows."
- **Hiring speed**: check whether headcount is outrunning proof of product appeal. Quote: "Hiring too fast is by far the biggest killer of startups that raise money."
- **Slow growth diagnosis**: the real reason is usually the product. Quote: "when founders have slow growth they don't want to face what is usually the real reason: the product is not appealing enough."
- **Staff is effect, not cause**: Quote: "the large staffs of successful startups are probably more the effect of growth than the cause."
- **Moderately appealing trap**: decent initial growth plus easy first round is not validation. Quote: "They make something moderately appealing and have decent initial growth."
- **Fix product, not headcount**: early on the product evolves rather than being built out. Quote: "At this early stage, the product needs to evolve more than to be"
- **Premature scaling (Buchheit)**: scaling a business with bad unit economics for impressive numbers. Quote: "founders take a small business that isn't really working (bad unit economics, typically) and then scale it up because they want impressive growth numbers."
- **Usage growth discounted**: investors discount predicted revenue, so usage-only metrics need higher growth. Quote: "if you're measuring usage you need a higher growth rate to impress investors."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- At current burn and recent growth rate, do you reach profitability before the money runs out? — founder does not know, or answers with "we will raise".
- What exactly is your plan B if you cannot raise, and on what date do you trigger it? — no written plan.
- What will each new hire change in growth? — hiring as a fix for slow growth.
- Why is growth only ok, not great? — any answer other than the product not being appealing enough gets scrutiny.
- What is your revenue growth per year? — under 5x with no profitability and reliance on investors.
- Are the unit economics working before you scale? — scaling a business that "isn't really working".

## Red flags / kill signals
- Default dead + slow growth + little time left (fatal pinch).
- Assuming it is easy to raise more money — "the more you depend on it, the falser it becomes."
- Hiring to boost growth of a moderately appealing product.
- High expenses with slow growth — "unappealing to investors".
- Investor pressure to overhire (kill-or-cure incentives of VCs differ from the founder's survival incentive).
- Scaling with bad unit economics for growth optics.

## Green flags
- Founder knows default alive/dead status and has checked early.
- Resists hiring urge: "YC's most successful companies have never been the fastest to hire" (Altman).
- Seeks growth via doing things that don't scale or redesigning the product.
- Written plan B with a trigger date.

## Named examples
- Airbnb: waited 4 months after YC funding before hiring its first employee, founders overworked evolving the product.
- Trevor Blackwell: built the default alive calculator.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the assumption that growing fast and operating cheaply are a sharp tradeoff.
- Even growth over 5x does not make fundraising more than plan A: "investors are so fickle".
- Not-raising does not fix overhiring: Graham likens avoiding funding to total abstinence to avoid alcoholism.
