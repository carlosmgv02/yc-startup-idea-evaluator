## One-line thesis
With AI competition back and no new step-change model, the way to find a great idea is to be contrarian and right: start from first principles and from users' desperate needs, treat "feels dangerous or weird" as a signal rather than a filter, and ignore rules like TAM, VC fundability or hot trends as a reason to say no.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Hot means derivative**: Check if the idea is chosen because it is hot. Quote: "If you only want to work on things that are hot, you're going to find yourself working on derivative ideas that end up being obvious"
- **Competitor count tail**: Quote: "number three through number 98 of all the people in that market, their startups are going to die."
- **Unique insight over vertical**: Quote: "it's becoming more important to think about what's your actual like unique insight that is going to enable you to find a good idea"
- **Contrarian bet**: Quote: "what's like the contrarian bet you're going to make"
- **Two-year window**: After a new platform, obvious ideas are picked over in roughly two years. Quote: "roughly two years after that the obvious ideas begin to have been picked over and you have to like look deeper for a secret."
- **Non-obvious feels dangerous**: Quote: "non-obvious feels dangerous and scary like I could devote my life to this waste 10 years on this and have no outcome."
- **Unexamined mental models**: Quote: "ways of thinking that are out there that we pick up in media or in like sort of the conversations we have with the people around us"
- **Dead bodies are not proof of a bad idea**: Check whether pre-AI failures are failures of the capability. Quote: "everyone else who has been in the space uh you know prior to the AI revolution has failed. So there just so many dead bodies stacked up around this idea."
- **Pull from the market**: Quote: "The customers are sort of pulling it out of him already."
- **Tarpit idea label**: Peers calling it a tarpit while customers say they need it. Quote: "They'll say like, oh, you're working on a tarpit idea."
- **Crowded spaces still win**: Quote: "Door Dash entered a very crowded space."
- **Gray area legal**: Quote: "a lot of great startup ideas are sort of in this gray area of like the law is not totally clear."
- **Fear as a signal**: Quote: "really really great founders sort of sense that as actually uh signal."
- **First principles on needs**: Quote: "you should think about from first principles what are the things that markets and people need"
- **Outdated laws**: Not illegal acts, but laws written before a tech shift. Quote: "finding laws that were written in a time before some big tech shift that changes everything and just don't reflect reality can be really valuable."
- **Do not simply break the law**: Quote: "don't just run out and do illegal things like that's actually clearly net bad"
- **Mitigate downsides**: Quote: "thinking from first principles also does actually involve thinking about well what are the downsides right um and how do we mitigate those things"
- **Consensus playbooks to flip**: Quote: "look at what are like emerging playbooks for ways to build startups that might be wrong or it might be time to flip them."
- **Compound startup**: Build the whole product when a point solution cannot be adopted. Quote: "instead of doing the standard SAS approach of let's just build the best point solution that's actually built the whole thing"
- **Codegen lowers switching cost**: Quote: "with codegen increasingly you can actually bring the switching cost closer to zero"
- **Time to value**: Quote: "you could have like time to value in like less than a month when it used to take a year."
- **Forward-deployed engineer playbook as target of flipping**: Quote: "judged by just what's become the most entrenched default playbook it might be the forward deployed engineer approach"
- **TAM is only an indicator**: Quote: "neither investors nor founders should use that as like cross it off the list"
- **Rules that talk you out of money**: Quote: "the more rules you have about investing, uh, the more ways you can basically talk yourself out of making a lot of money in ventures."
- **Powers and moats are not a gate**: Quote: "it would be the stupidest thing in the world to use that as your only criteria for whether or not you should work on a company."
- **Focus on customer need and severity**: Quote: "if you're just really really focused on people's problems and how severe those problems are, like you know, you'll figure out the rest."
- **Get out of the chair**: Quote: "you can't sit in your chair sit sitting in front of a computer and uh divine these things per se. You actually have to get out of the house."
- **Work backwards from growth goal**: Quote: "how do I work backwards from my growth goal?"
- **VC feedback as noise**: Quote: "they would have told him like, "Oh, this isn't VC fundable, selling to local governments, hardware, like you should do B2B SAS.""
- **Sci-fi founder**: Ideas scary because hard. Quote: "ideas that most people are scared to build because they're just so freaking hard."
- **Outcomes over papers**: Quote: "the thing that uh really great builders optimized for is like outcomes for customers and users"
- **9 of 10 / 1 of 10**: Quote: "nine out of 10 people might tell you you're stupid or crazy but then one out of 10 people might be exactly the person who believes what you believe"
- **Verifiable sources**: Quote: "if it's coming from users coming from your own personal experience or the experience that of people who you directly talk to uh great"
- **N equals 1**: Quote: "we're all N equals 1."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What do you know that most people around you do not believe? — consensus idea from media or X.
- How many funded startups are already in this vertical, and what is your secret? — "workflow to automate" with no insight.
- Are customers asking for it already? — no pull ("I need that tomorrow" never heard).
- Which default playbook in your space have you chosen to flip? — followed the standard playbook with no reason.
- Who told you it would not work, and what were their premises? — no examination of premises.
- Which law or constraint was written before the tech shift, and what happens if it changes? — "it is legal because everyone says so" or deliberate illegality with no downside plan.
- What is the severity of the problem for the person who has it? — no concrete people.
- If a codegen-driven approach lowers switching cost, what does your time to value look like? — months of custom work per customer.
- Why was the TAM argument not a reason to quit? — founder gave up due to a TAM spreadsheet.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Choosing the idea because the topic is hot or on X/TechCrunch.
- "Another vertical AI agent for insurance or banking" with several startups already and no unique insight.
- Using VC fundability, hardware, small TAM or Atlanta location as the reason to drop a strong need.
- Rule-only evaluation (the seven powers as the sole criterion).
- Deliberately doing "illegal things" for its own sake.
- Following the forward-deployed engineer or full-stack playbook just because it is the default.
- Dismissing market pull because friends at a party disapprove.

## Green flags
- Customers pulling the product out of the founder, even for a weird idea.
- Founders who treat discomfort or danger as a signal worth investigating.
- A contrarian bet with a first-principles explanation (DoorDash marketplace over full stack; Coinbase working with banks).
- Switching cost near zero thanks to code generation or an AI forward-deployed engineer.
- Founders who go out and talk to customers, work backwards from growth goals and change go-to-market.
- Severe human impact (Flock safety: crimes solved).

## Named examples
- DoorDash: entered a crowded delivery space after Postmates, Seamless and Order Ahead; bet on marketplace instead of full-stack kitchens (SpoonRocket, Sprig).
- Zimride, Lyft and Ridejoy: Lyft shifted from long-distance to short rides with smartphones; founders worried about jail.
- Uber, Instacart: non-obvious winners from the iPhone era.
- Coinbase and Brian Armstrong: worked with banks and regulators while the crypto crowd was anarchist; KYC makes the product worse but widens the market.
- OpenAI: negative press, papers critique, scaling laws; SpaceX with reusable rockets: negative press and failed launches.
- Flock Safety (Garrett Langley): licence plate cameras, hardware, small TAM of "$50 or $60 million a year", Atlanta; now "$7.5 billion"; growth via police departments and local news.
- Campfire: AI-native ERP built whole to compete with NetSuite.
- Gigaml [sic: "gig ML"]: AI forward-deployed engineer that does in minutes what human FDEs do in weeks.
- Palantir: the original forward-deployed model; Bob McGrew skeptical of overuse.
- Parker Conrad, Rippling: compound startup, took about two years to the first version.
- Plaid and open banking: regulatory capture by large banks.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "avoid tarpit ideas" when customers pull from a space with dead bodies, though tarpit is cited as a label peers use.
- Nuances "don't build the whole product / ship small": compound startups are now feasible, but "typically" that advice stands.
- Nuances "do things that are legal": gray areas are valuable only where the law is outdated, not where it says you cannot.
- Contradicts TAM-first investing: a "$50 million" TAM Flock ended at $7.5 billion; yet the speakers say "it's merely an indicator".
- Forward-deployed engineers are both the entrenched default and the thing to flip; Bob McGrew thinks it is overused.
- Light Cone's own advice is "N equals 1": the speakers say not to over-trust them either.
