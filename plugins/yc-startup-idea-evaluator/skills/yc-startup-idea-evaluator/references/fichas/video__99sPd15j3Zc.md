## One-line thesis
Pure software is too easy to replicate now, so durability comes from "the hard bit" (regulation, atoms, brutal sales, network effects, distribution); founders should launch early, talk to customers and bet on people.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **The hard bit test**: your startup must have something hard that is not just writing software. Quote: "your startup has to have a hard bit and 10 years ago writing a lot of software was in and of itself the hard bit that is not hard anymore"
- **Examples of hard bits**: Quote: "brutally hard B2B sales into some industries others it might be a regulatory barrier like getting a banking license almost killed me"
- **Avoid the easy end of the spectrum**: Quote: "I just shy away from the easier end of the spectrum and just like pick bits that are harder and more ambitious because it will make your startup more durable"
- **Pure software is easy to replicate**: Quote: "I think pure software is just not um it's just too easy to replicate now so just ask yourself where's the hard bit"
- **Network effect exception**: Quote: "maybe Partful actually has a network effect"
- **Cheaper to build means less defensible**: Quote: "if you are just producing the same software that people produced 5 years ago you can absolutely do that for way less money now but unfortunately it is um much much less durable and defensible"
- **Capital need rises with ambition**: Quote: "startup ambitious startup founders will just try and do harder and harder things and that will require in a lot of not all cases but a lot of cases capital"
- **Thin wrapper is not a verdict**: Quote: "some of the biggest companies today are AI rapper 100% before then they were SQL rappers or S3 rappers"
- **Wedge = technical depth or distribution**: Quote: "having a deep technical wedge can be really important or distribution can be really important"
- **Distribution is a hard thing now**: Quote: "software no longer is the hard thing about building the company distribution can be one of the hard things"
- **Early stage differentiation**: white-glove service. Quote: "the way that you stand out from them to an individual customer is by giving them like real like white glove service"
- **Launch early**: Quote: "the biggest um regret that YC founders have after the batch is not having launched soon enough"
- **Startups are empirical**: Quote: "startups are like an extremely empirical exercise where you need to come up with a hypothesis very very quickly and then run a test with the real world"
- **Bottleneck cadence**: Quote: "the best founders every two weeks we meet them it's a new problem because they've overwhelmed the last bottleneck"
- **Comfort bias**: Quote: "a lot of founders are a lot more comfortable building than they are talking to customers"
- **Pivot test**: pivot only on real evidence, not lost enthusiasm. Quote: "it's only when I've got real evidence that my hypothesis that the like fundamental of what I was trying to build was in fact the wrong thing that you should pivot"
- **Customer-pulled pivots**: Quote: "those pivots where your customers pull the pivot out of you tend to be easier ones to make the decision about making"
- **Co-founder choice**: Quote: "the biggest mistake I've seen is technical co-founders thinking they need a business co-founder"
- **Solo founders statistically worse**: Quote: "we keep funding them and you know some do well but statistically they do worse"
- **Bet on people**: Quote: "at the end of the day I think YC funds people I think this is the biggest mo like by a wide margin"
- **Do not delegate customer conversations**: Quote: "talking to customers i think that is what kept me focused on what was important and it's the last thing that I would trust"
- **AI-native company: start with one narrow loop**: Quote: "I'd pick a single loop basically"
- **Cost of intelligence**: Quote: "it's a 10x cost reduction per year roughly speaking for the same intelligence"
- **Community**: Quote: "if you are the only one in a in a city it's very difficult because you don't have any peers to uh push you forward"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is the hard bit in your company? — Red flag: "the software" is the answer.
- Where do you see yourself on the easy-hard spectrum, and who would clone you in a month? — Red flag: a Calendly-style product.
- What did you learn from users this fortnight, and what is the next bottleneck? — Red flag: research or building continues for weeks without customer contact.
- Why a solo founder, and who pulls you out when you are low? — Red flag: no support structure.
- Is your distribution plan more than "build it"? — Red flag: assumes software quality will carry itself.
- What is the evidence that makes you pivot instead of enthusiasm running out? — Red flag: pivot motivated by sadness.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Products that a model plus a weekend can replicate (Calendly, DocuSign-type [sic "docysine"] examples named as bad starts today).
- Staying in the library: "sitting in a library and thinking really hard" does not translate to startup success.
- Early-decision applicant who does not explain why now.
- Pivoting from something you know deeply into a shallow space: "the grass will not be greener".

## Green flags
- Regulatory, physical or sales barriers (banking license, nuclear reactors, spaceflight).
- Frequent launches, weekly user conversations, new bottleneck each fortnight.
- Co-founder trusted for integrity and morale: "someone who you know can help make you happy when you're sad".
- High agency built through progressively harder side projects.

## Named examples
- GoCardless: sports-team payments pivot into broader payment collection (customer-pulled).
- Brex founders: applied with cardboard VR sunglasses; "if the founding team is strong enough it just doesn't matter".
- Cursor founder's Hacker News launch history; a small-nuclear-reactor founder needing about $800 million.
- Solo-founder self-improving-agents experiment: founder "got sad" six weeks into the batch.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "one-person billion-dollar company" optimism: adding people has low marginal cost versus benefit at small scale (though AI will compress companies below Dunbar's number).
- Contradicts "complementary skills": "the biggest mistake is technical co-founders looking for business co-founders".
- Panelists disagree: YC itself says "maybe 30%" of what one partner funds another would not.
- Panel partners differ on whether technical depth is still required (one admits re-evaluating).
