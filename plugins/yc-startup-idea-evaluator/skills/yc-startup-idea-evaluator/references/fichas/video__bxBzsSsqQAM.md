## One-line thesis
Moats come after you find a painful problem and win with speed; the seven powers from Hamilton Helmer's book (process power, cornered resource, switching costs, counter-positioning, brand, network economies, scale economies) all have AI-era versions, but they are defensive and only worth thinking about once you have something to defend.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Moats come later**: do not reject an idea for lacking a visible long-term moat. Quote: "it would be like pretty dumb for somebody to decide not to work on a startup idea because they can't see what the long-term motes of that idea could be"
- **Do not use moats to choose between ideas**: Quote: "you shouldn't do that. Like a moat is inherently a defensive thing and you have to have something to defend."
- **Start with the problem**: Quote: "you should just find a problem and go solve it."
- **Speed as the first moat**: Quote: "the early stages at the beginning, the only mote that startups have is really just speed."
- **Speed vs incumbents**: Cursor ran one-day sprints; big companies take weeks to years. Quote: "his product development cycle for shipping features and sprint cycles were one day."
- **When to start worrying**: after rapid growth and proving the use case. Quote: "you find any sort of struck gold, then you keep digging."
- **Process power / hardness gap**: hackathon version vs mission-critical reliability. Quote: "the version you build in a hackathon isn't useful to anyone."
- **Last mile as moat**: 80% with 20% effort vs 99% accuracy. Quote: "you can probably build the 80% solution with 20% of the effort. But for these solutions and products to work, you need the 99% accuracy one"
- **Schlep blindness**: Quote: "the last like 10% of getting it to work reliably across like tens of thousands of KYC requests like per day is sort of like a particular type of painstaking drudgery work"
- **Cornered resource via forward-deployed work**: real data and real workflows from customers who would not normally get good software. Quote: "they're going out and getting a cornered resource in the form of real data and real workflows."
- **Own model is not the only moat**: Quote: "Turns out that's not true. Turns out that's just one of the possible moats."
- **Context engineering is enough early**: Quote: "even if just context engineering gets you 80 or 90% of the way there, that's plenty."
- **Switching costs, AI flavor**: long pilots lead to seven-figure contracts via customization of agent logic. Quote: "these pilots are so long is because they're very much building custom software for the specific operations in these companies."
- **AI also lowers switching costs**: Quote: "LLMs might significantly reduce the switching cost cuz the LLMs can figure out how to like morph the data from the old schema into the new schema."
- **Consumer memory as switching cost**: Quote: "memory is already becoming a bit of a switching cost for me."
- **Counter-positioning definition**: Quote: "doing something that is difficult for the incumbent that you were competing with to copy because it would cannibalize their business."
- **Per-seat pricing Achilles heel**: Quote: "if their AI agents do a good job and actually work, those companies will need fewer employees doing this work"
- **Outcome-based pricing**: Quote: "so much of the startups pricing models are around sort of like work delivered or tasks completed."
- **Second mover advantage**: Quote: "we have seen over and over again that like advantage to being the second mover in the space."
- **Wallet share**: AI agents take labor budget, not software budget. Quote: "they can get the HVAC people to pay them uh actually for the customer support piece, which is not 1% of their spend, but 4 to 10% of their spend."
- **Network effects as data and evals**: Quote: "evals is where you get a lot of the this workflow work or they didn't work and then take that back and iterate and improve your contact engineering."
- **Scale economies at app layer**: rare; Exa crawls once and reuses for many customers. Quote: "once you crawl a big chunk of the web, you can reuse that same crawl for for many different customers."
- **Pain intensity**: Quote: "It's not like a oh, it'd be nice if I could do this. It's a oh, I am not going to get promoted this year."
- **Brand caution**: Quote: "it just takes time to acquire brand."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Whose existential pain is this ("I'll get fired")? — "it'd be nice to have".
- If someone built your demo in a weekend hackathon, what is the 99% version they cannot copy? — nothing beyond the demo.
- How fast do you ship versus the incumbent or the big labs? — slower than a big company.
- What real workflow or data do you own from sitting with customers? — none, pure prompt wrapper.
- Does the incumbent's business model block them from copying you (per-seat pricing cannibalization)? — they can bolt it on easily.
- If the customer switched tomorrow, what would they lose? — nothing; or you rely only on data lock-in LLMs can now migrate.
- What does it cost an LLM agent customer to replace you after a long pilot? — pilots not converting.
- What budget are you tapping: software or labor spend? — competing for a small software budget.
- Does usage improve your product (evals, private data)? — no feedback loop.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Founder picks among ideas by forecasting 5-year moats, or abandons an idea for lacking one.
- Demo-grade product only, "ChatGPT wrapper" without hard last-mile work.
- Incumbent can add AI to its existing seat-based product without cannibalizing itself.
- Slow shipping relative to incumbents (speed is the early moat).
- Reliance on data-migration switching costs that LLMs now erode.
- Relying on brand as an early-stage startup moat.

## Green flags
- Specific person with severe, existential pain.
- One-day shipping cycles.
- Forward-deployed engineers sitting with customers to capture real workflows, leading to long pilots that convert to seven-figure contracts.
- Hard, mission-critical, edge-case-heavy verticals (KYC, loan origination) that require specialized knowledge.
- Pricing on work delivered or tasks completed.
- Product that works better out of the box than incumbents, with faster onboarding.
- Product with superhuman ability (200 languages, infinite patience).
- Usage flywheel that improves evals or models.

## Named examples
- Cursor: one-day sprints; data from users trains tab autocomplete; speed as early moat.
- Windsurf (Varun): "the only moat is speed".
- Case Text (Jake Heller), Greenlight (KYC for banks), Casca (loan origination): process power in mission-critical agents.
- Plaid: thousands of integrations as process power; code gen to add institutions faster.
- Scale AI, Palantir: cornered resource via DoD access.
- Character AI: fine-tuned models cut serving cost by 10x.
- Happy Robot (DHL), Salient (banks): long pilots to custom workflows, switching costs.
- Avoca (HVAC, vs ServiceTitan): wallet share 1% to 4-10%.
- Legora vs Harvey: second mover focused on application layer rather than fine-tuning.
- Giga ML: product works out of the box, faster onboarding vs customer-support decacorns.
- Speak vs Duolingo: counter-positioning against a gamification app.
- OpenAI ChatGPT vs Google Gemini: brand and counter-positioning (ads cash cow).
- Exa, Channel 3, Orange Slice: web crawl as scale economy.
- Stripe, DoorDash: second movers that won.

## Tensions
- A Light Cone refrain "make something people want" vs. noting that Helmer's business-school framework is now essential reading.
- Peter Thiel's "competition is for losers" is cited for why moats are existential, yet the advice is to ignore moats at the start.
- Many assumed owning a model was the only AI moat; the speakers say it is not.
- AI lowers switching costs for challengers while creating new ones (agent customization).
- Founder-controlled incumbents may cannibalize themselves; non-founder-controlled ones likely cannot.
- Scale economies are mostly at the model layer, "not actually played out that much at the application layer".
