## One-line thesis
Nearly all failure funnels through one mistake, not making something users want, and the 18 listed mistakes are the ways founders end up there.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Single failure mode**: Quote: "In a sense there's just one mistake that kills startups: not making something users want."
- **Single founder**: few successful startups have one founder; it signals no friend would join and removes the "I can't let my friends down" force. Quote: "It probably means the founder couldn't talk any of his friends into starting the company with him."
- **Marginal niche**: small obscure niches chosen to avoid competition are a mistake. Quote: "You can only avoid competition by avoiding good ideas."
- **Think of ideas for someone else**: Quote: "What would be a great idea for someone else to do as a startup?"
- **Derivative idea**: start from a specific unsolved problem, not from a company to imitate. Quote: "Instead of starting from companies and working back to the problems they solved, look for problems and imagine the company that might solve them."
- **Personal problem as best source**: Quote: "It seems like the best problems to solve are ones that affect you personally."
- **Obstinacy vs thrash**: reuse test for pivots; if each new idea reuses most of the previous build, the process converges. Quote: "If in each new idea you're able to re-use most of what you built for the previous ones, then you're probably in a process that converges."
- **Users as pivot test**: Quote: "If you're thinking about turning in some new direction and your users seem excited about it, it's probably a good bet."
- **Non-technical hiring risk**: business founders cannot tell good programmers apart. Quote: "business guys can't tell which are the good programmers."
- **Launch fast**: Quote: "Launching too slowly has probably killed a hundred times more startups than launching too fast"
- **Minimum launch core**: Quote: "identify a core that's both (a) useful on its own and (b) something that can be incrementally expanded into the whole project, and then get that done as soon as possible."
- **Specific user in mind**: Quote: "if you're trying to solve problems you don't understand, you're hosed."
- **Name-ten-users test**: when building for others, talk specific ones into using it. Quote: "you have to be able to talk some specific ones into using what you're making. If you can't, you're on the wrong track."
- **Runway to next step**: raise enough to reach a visibly higher level (prototype, launch, growth); suggested buffer. Quote: "You should take more than you think you'll need, maybe 50% to 100% more, because software takes longer to write and deals longer to close than you expect."
- **Hiring rules**: Quote: "only hire people who are either going to write code or go out and get users, because those are the only things you need at first."
- **Too much money**: harder to change direction once a team is built around one plan. Quote: "once you take a lot of money it gets harder to change direction."
- **Take first reasonable deal**: Quote: "Economically, startups are an all-or-nothing game. Bargain-hunting among investors is a waste of time."
- **Wealth before money**: delay the business model, solve the core problem. Quote: "the core problem in a startup is how to create wealth (= how much people want something x the number who want it), not how to convert that wealth into money."
- **Business model proportion**: Quote: "It is irresponsible not to think about business models. It's just ten times more irresponsible not to think about the product."
- **Get hands dirty**: at least one founder must do business and find users. Quote: "If you want to start a startup, you have to face the fact that you can't just hack."
- **Founder fights**: choose people carefully, do not suppress misgivings. Quote: "Don't suppress misgivings. It's much easier to fix problems before the company is started than after."
- **Founder departure base rate**: Quote: "About 20% of the startups we've funded have had a founder leave."
- **Half-hearted effort**: Quote: "Most startups fail because they don't make something people want, and the reason most don't is that they don't try hard enough."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Who exactly is the user, and can you name specific ones who will use it? — "teenagers", "business users", no names.
- Are you the target market? If not, how do you measure users' response? — guessing instead of measuring.
- Where did the idea come from: a specific unsolved problem or an existing company? — imitation with variation.
- Why this niche? Is it small to dodge competition? — marginal niche chosen for safety.
- What is the smallest core that is useful alone and expandable? — plans a big-bang launch.
- Why have you not launched? — vague excuses; "would they still be waiting if software were 100% finished?"
- Who writes the code and who judges its quality? — non-technical team that cannot evaluate programmers.
- Have you started with your cofounder knowing each other well, and what misgivings do you have? — suppressed doubts, housemate cofounder.
- Are you full-time? — day job kept.
- How much runway and what does it get you to? — raising less than needed for the next step.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Single founder (vote of no confidence, no one to hold the low points).
- Marginal niche chosen to avoid competition.
- Derivative copy of an existing company.
- Obstinate attachment to the original plan, or switching ideas every week.
- Business-founder-led startup that outsources programming to hires.
- Wrong platform choices that look responsible to outsiders.
- Launch delayed by perfectionism, fear of users, fear of judgment.
- No specific user, "someone will want it".
- Hiring a lot early.
- Raising VC-scale money before direction is settled.
- Founders who dismiss users in favor of an early monetization plan.
- Founders avoiding "business stuff" (selling, finding users, deals).
- Cofounder picked out of convenience or guilt.
- Day job kept, half-hearted effort.

## Green flags
- Idea from the founders' own unsolved problem.
- Two or three committed cofounders who like each other.
- Early launch of a useful core to tolerant early adopters.
- Users excited about a new direction.
- One founder doing deals with executives despite how painful (the most successful of the first YC batch "by an order of magnitude").
- Cheap prototype-first plan with minimal spend.
- Full-time commitment.

## Named examples
- Viaweb: made-up user problem, but built with eyes open.
- Apple (Wozniak), Google (Larry and Sergey), Hotmail (Bhatia and Smith): problems that affected the founders.
- Hotmail and PayPal: platform choice (FreeBSD/Unix vs Windows).
- Java applets: platform that "killed just about 100% of the startups who believed that".
- Google: made search work first, money later; first tried to sell the algorithm.
- Blogger: survived a founder leaving.
- YouTube/Google: acquirers pay for users, not cleverness.
- Apple board firing Steve Jobs: investor mismanagement.

## Tensions
- Business model: tells founders to "leave business models for later", while stating it is irresponsible not to think about them.
- Obstinacy vs openness: neither extreme is fine; reuse test for convergence.
- Fundraising: too little money kills, too much money also kills.
- Says to take the first reasonable VC deal, yet footnote 8 says push back on artificially low valuations.
- Founder departure (20%) is survivable, yet single founders are a red flag.
- Notes it only lists causes you control; ineptitude and bad luck are excluded.
