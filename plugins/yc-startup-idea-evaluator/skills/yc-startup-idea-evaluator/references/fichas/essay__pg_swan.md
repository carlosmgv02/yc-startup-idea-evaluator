## One-line thesis
Startup returns are concentrated in a few huge winners, and the best ideas look like bad ideas at first, so investors (and evaluators) must resist safe-looking picks and fundraising as a signal.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Evaluate for outlier size, not probability of success**: ask whether it could succeed really big, not whether it will work at all. Quote: "it's not their chances of succeeding that matter but their chances of succeeding really big."
- **Power law of outcomes**: expect effectively all value in one company. Quote: "effectively all the returns are concentrated in a few big winners"
- **Quantified concentration at YC**: Dropbox and Airbnb are about three quarters of YC portfolio value. Quote: "The total value of the companies we've funded is around 10 billion, give or take a few. But just two companies, Dropbox and Airbnb, account for about three quarters of"
- **Variation of outcomes**: Quote: "we are just not prepared for the 1000x variation in outcomes that one finds in startup investing."
- **Big-hit probability is not a fraction of success probability**: do not rank by likelihood of modest success. Quote: "The probability that a startup will make it big is not simply a constant fraction of the probability that they will succeed at all."
- **Ignore the elephant**: do not be seduced by obvious competence. Quote: "You have to ignore the elephant in front of you, the likelihood they'll succeed, and focus instead on the separate and almost invisibly intangible question of whether they'll succeed really"
- **Good-seeming-bad-idea intersection**: look for ideas that seem bad but are good. Quote: "The intersection is the sweet spot for startups."
- **Most bad-seeming ideas are bad**: the Thiel Venn diagram reminds that the intersection is small. Quote: "It also reminds you that the vast majority of ideas that seem bad are bad."
- **If it were obviously good, someone would have done it**: Quote: "if a good idea were obviously good, someone else would already have done it."
- **Perfect-bad-idea pattern (Facebook)**: niche market, no money, something that does not matter, yet it won. Quote: "a site (1) for a niche market (2) with no money (3) to do something that didn't matter."
- **Fundraising is a misleading signal**: do not use investor appetite as a proxy for quality. Quote: "fundraising is not merely a useless metric, but positively misleading."
- **Do not look through the investors' eyes**: ignore "what will investors think?" if the founders are good. Quote: "who cares what investors think?"
- **Pitch cannot rescue a disliked idea**: fundraising depends on what is sold and how well. Quote: "even the most convincing pitch can't sell an idea that investors don't like."
- **Founders can feel wrong**: smart 19-year-olds unsure what to build may beat the safer group on big-win probability. Quote: "the probability that those 19 year olds will might be higher than that of the other, safer group."
- **Measure effect**: measuring what is wrong optimizes it. Quote: "if you start measuring something you start optimizing it, and I know it's the wrong thing to optimize."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- If this works, how big can it get? — red flag: best case is a good small business
- Why does this idea sound bad, and why is it good anyway? — red flag: sounds bad and has no thesis for why it is good
- Is this attractive only because it is safe and legible to investors? — red flag: the main argument is that investors will like it
- What is the 10,000x scenario? — red flag: none
- What do you see that most people (including smart ones) think is wrong? — red flag: consensus-friendly idea

## Red flags / kill signals
- Idea that seems good to everyone, e.g. an obviously good idea — "someone else would already have done it"
- Seems bad and is bad — "the vast majority of ideas that seem bad are bad"; sounding bad is necessary, not sufficient
- Judging the team on fundraising results — inversely correlated with finding big winners per the essay
- Only incremental upside ("even if they succeed the effect on your returns will be insignificant")

## Green flags
- Good founders pursuing an idea that looks lame, niche or unprofitable but has a strong underlying thesis
- Idea sounds like a bad idea to most smart people yet the founders understand something specific
- Willingness to ignore investor opinion

## Named examples
- Facebook: sounded lame to PG, "A site for college students to waste time?"
- Airbnb: PG worried they might not raise; "who cares what investors think?"; Fred Wilson would not fund; Sequoia contact Greg McAdoo understood vacation rentals
- Dropbox and Airbnb: about three quarters of YC portfolio value
- Microsoft and Apple: could be described in the same terms as Facebook (niche, no money, not important)
- Google: counter-example, seemed bad because many search engines already existed (note 3)
- Peter Thiel: Venn diagram of "seems like a bad idea" and "is a good idea"
- Reddit: made little money for YC but large effect on world

## Tensions
- Contradicts risk-averse screening: accept lower probability of success for higher chance of a huge outcome.
- Contradicts "investors liked it, so it must be good": fundraising is inversely correlated.
- Nuance: not all big hits looked bad (Google, note 3). The essay is also about investor economics; for a founder, "Some want to take over the world, and some just want that first few million" (note 1).
- Author admits he cannot always follow his own rule ("I can lay out what I know to be the right thing to do, and still not do it").
