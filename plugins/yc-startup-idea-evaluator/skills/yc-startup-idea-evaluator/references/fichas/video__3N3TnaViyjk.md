## One-line thesis
DoorDash validated both sides of a marketplace cheaply by doing every delivery itself, bet on an unserved segment (suburbs) because customers said the need was there, and won on retention and frequency, not on capital.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Founder-market chemistry plus project fit**: two keep-going criteria: you enjoy working together and you like the idea enough. Quote: "if we decided that we enjoyed working together, we're going to keep going. And two um was whether or not we actually liked the idea of the project enough"
- **Live the customer's day**: shadow the merchant instead of surveying; customers cannot articulate their problems. Quote: "Sometimes it's very very hard for any customer to tell you exactly what is inside their brain when you ask them what problems they have."
- **Follow the thread of a turned-down behavior**: the idea came from a merchant's booklet of refused delivery orders, a demand she was ignoring. Quote: "she had showed us a booklet of orders she had turned down. All of them were delivery orders."
- **Three marketplace questions before building**: will consumers pay, will merchants pay, does a driver supply exist. Quote: "whether or not consumers would pay us for the service."
- **Retention without incentives as the organic check**: customers who return without ads, coupons or discounts. Quote: "they kept coming back in again without our throwing advertising at them, coupons or discounts at them."
- **Do the supply-side work manually first**: post Craigslist ads, see who shows up, segment the workers; test whether supply is a different population. Quote: "we would just post ads on Craigslist, see who shows up, and then started filtering different segments of drivers."
- **Cheap supply experiment**: two groups of 20 drivers offered $25/hour guaranteed to switch; only one of 40 said yes, showing a different, self-selected labor pool. Quote: "Exactly one driver out of two groups of 20, so one out of 40 said yes."
- **Market creation vs incumbents' market**: size the unserved set, not the served one. Quote: "There's about rough math, a million restaurants um in the US. Only 20,000 of them offer delivery."
- **Demand outrunning supply is lethal**: a marketplace needs a way to throttle demand; DoorDash had no way to shut off orders and every delivery was late. Quote: "when you have no ability to fulfill the demand or to shut off, you know, the website that kept receiving these orders when you didn't have enough drivers on the road."
- **Contrarian segment from customer need, then verified in the P&L**: suburbs over dense cities because need was higher; line-by-line unit economics later confirmed it (bigger baskets, easier parking, single-family homes). Quote: "Listen to the customer, run the test."
- **Retention and frequency as the competitive weapon**: hardest metrics to fake; superior retention makes every dollar go further than rivals. Quote: "one of the hardest things to to really fool is the retention and the engagement of a of a product."
- **Everyone does the work**: keep founders in the operation to gain a point of view. Quote: "every person at Door Dash, myself included, does deliveries every year."
- **Expertise by doing**: Quote: "There's no better way to be the expert than just to do the work."
- **Start with the ugly MVP**: a static HTML page, eight PDF menus and a Google Voice number. Quote: "all it was was a static uh HTML page with eight PDF menus"
- **Seed investors bet on team and market creation when data is thin**: 10 weeks of data is a conviction bet. Quote: "it was 10 weeks of data. And so at the end of the day, if you were an investor looking at Door Dash on demo day"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Have you done the fulfilment yourself, and what did you learn about each step? — never touched the operation.
- What evidence do you have that each side of the marketplace will pay or participate? — assumed, not tested.
- Do customers return without discounts? — retention only exists with incentives.
- Is the supply pool the same people as an existing platform's, or a new population? — assumes poaching with higher pay.
- What happens when demand spikes beyond supply? — no throttle, no refund plan.
- Which market is everyone ignoring that your customers keep pointing to? — consensus geography only.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Idea with no customer pain, only "project" enjoyment (the tablet survey app met one criterion only and was dropped).
- Relying on surveys instead of living the workflow.
- Demand without ability to fulfil or to shut off orders — "no one usually complains of too much demand except when you have no ability to fulfill."
- Assuming supply must be paid the most to join — the 25 vs 20 dollar test disproved this for a different segment.

## Green flags
- Organic repeat usage with no marketing spend, even while unable to raise money.
- Superior retention and frequency versus competitors.
- Unit economics that improve line by line in the contrarian segment.
- Founders still delivering orders themselves years later.
- Instinct to refund customers at 40% of the bank account after a failure.

## Named examples
- DoorDash: began as Palo Alto Delivery, static page, founders did deliveries; suburbs bet; series C down round and three years of rejections (2016-2018) before series D in 2018; category leader by 2019.
- Competitors: GrubHub and Seamless worked with the 20,000 restaurants with their own fleets; Uber Eats; price war in dense cities.
- COVID: commissions cut by half and TV campaign promoting the whole industry, justified by a long-term mission; merchants had 17 days of cash on hand.

## Tensions
- Contradicts spreadsheet-driven geography choice ("order density needed in city centers"): the suburbs won on need and, later, on unit economics.
- Nuances "investors read data": three years of rejections despite strong internal metrics because the winner was not obvious in a crowded, better-capitalized field.
- Speaker admits survivor bias in the customer-obsessed story.
