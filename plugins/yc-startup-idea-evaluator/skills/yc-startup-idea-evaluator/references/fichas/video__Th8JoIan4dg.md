## One-line thesis
Nobody knows for sure which ideas win, but you can stack the deck: avoid the four common idea mistakes, test the idea against ten questions (founder-market fit first), favor hard, boring, competitive spaces, and ultimately launch to find out.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Solution in search of a problem (SISP)**: reject ideas that start from a technology ("AI is cool"). Quote: "if you do that you'll probably find a problem but it will it will be a superficially plausible problem it'll be a made-up problem that people don't really care about"
- **Start with a high-quality problem**: Quote: "you want to fall in love with a problem the best way to find a startup idea is to start with a high quality problem"
- **Specific and tractable, not abstract**: global-poverty-size problems are poor starting points. Quote: "they're too abstract to make good starting points for startup ideas you need something that's more specific something that's tractable with a startup"
- **Tarpit test**: common idea, seems easy, structural reason it is hard. Quote: "there's actually a structural reason why it's very hard or impossible to solve which is why after all these years no one has solved it"
- **Google the idea**: Quote: "it's amazing how many Founders skip the step of just like Googling for their own startup idea to see who has worked on it in the past"
- **Talk to past attempters**: Quote: "try to figure out what the hard part of this idea is that has caused other people to not be able to solve it yet"
- **Neither first idea nor perfect idea**: Quote: "you should think of your idea as a good starting point"
- **Q1 founder-market fit (most important)**: Quote: "founder market fit is just are you the right team to be working on this idea"
- **Pick ideas for your team**: Quote: "it doesn't matter if something is a good startup idea for someone else if it's not a good idea for your team"
- **Q2 market size**: billion dollar now, or small but rapidly growing (Coinbase 2012). Quote: "there are two kinds of markets for startups that are good um ones that are big now and ones that are small but rapidly growing"
- **Q3 acute problem**: the alternative is nothing. Quote: "if the alternative to your solution is literally nothing that's what a good problem looks like"
- **Q4 competition is good**: Quote: "most good startup ideas have competition but if you were going up against especially entrenched competition you typically need a new insight"
- **Q5 want it personally / know someone who does**: Quote: "if that's the case you definitely got to worry that you know maybe nobody wants this"
- **Q6 why now**: Quote: "something has recently changed in the world like a new technology regulatory change or a new problem that is often what creates a new opportunity"
- **Q7 proxies**: a large company doing something similar, not a direct competitor, in another geography or segment. Quote: "a proxy is a large company that does something similar to your startup but it is not a direct competitor"
- **Q8 want to work on it for years (nuanced)**: Quote: "if you're actually running a successful business you tend to become passionate about it over time"
- **Q9 scalable**: software yes, services trouble. Quote: "the place for Founders most often get into trouble here is with Services businesses like agencies or Dev shops anything that requires like high skill human labor in order to"
- **Q10 idea space hit rate**: Quote: "different idea spaces have wildly different hit rates"
- **Idea space data**: Quote: "if you started a company that did like fintech infrastructure or vertical SAS for Enterprise the the probability that your company became a billion dollar company was astonishingly High"
- **Idea spaces change**: Quote: "spaces flipped from hot to cold over time"
- **Fertile space enables drift**: Fivetran pivoted twice in the same space. Quote: "they put themselves in a good position to like bump into a good startup idea"
- **Hard-to-start ideas have less competition**: Stripe schlep. Quote: "those things seemed so hard that they scared off all the other people who might have started stripe"
- **Boring space has higher hit rate**: Quote: "boring ideas like payroll software have a much higher hit rate than fun ideas like apps to find new restaurants to eat at"
- **Fun at idea stage is not fun at month 12**: Quote: "how fun the initial idea sounded actually has little to no correlation with how much fun you will actually be having working on your company"
- **Existing competitors beat none**: Quote: "when founders go into spaces with no existing competitors they often find out that the reason that there are no competitors is because no one wants the product"
- **Competitors exist but are unused**: Dropbox, 20 competitors, most people used none. Quote: "even though there were like 20 companies doing this most people didn't use any of them and that strongly suggests that there actually was a problem here but the existing"
- **Insight vs entrenched incumbents**: Quote: "he integrated directly into the host operating system he could just sync your files automatically without you having to do anything"
- **Organic ideas outperform brainstormed**: Quote: "at least 70 percent of them have their startup ideas organically rather than by like sitting down and explicitly trying to think of a startup idea"
- **Brainstormed ideas drift into tarpits**: Quote: "they're especially likely to think of the same set of tar pit ideas"
- **Recipe: team expertise**: Quote: "any idea you come up with this way has automatic founder market fit"
- **Recipe: problem seen from an unusual position**: Quote: "what problems did you come across what did you learn that other people don't know"
- **Wish it existed is dangerous**: Quote: "is there a reason why this thing doesn't exist yet"
- **Recipe: recent change**: Quote: "look for things in the world that have changed recently that might have created a new opportunity"
- **Systematic search in an idea space**: A to B talked to truckers at truck stops. Quote: "they would basically talk to like anyone who knew anything about Trucking that was willing to talk to them"
- **Launch to know**: Quote: "the only way to know for sure if your startup idea is good is to Just Launch it and find out"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Why is this team the right one for this idea? — red flag: idea picked in the abstract, no expertise or experience
- Is this a real problem or did you start from the technology? — red flag: "AI is cool, what can I apply it to?"; users do not care when asked
- What is the alternative today? — red flag: users are fine with existing solutions or do nothing and do not mind
- Who has tried this before and what is the structural reason it is hard? — red flag: never Googled it; classic tarpit (friends meet-up app)
- What changed recently that makes this possible or necessary? — red flag: nothing changed
- Is there a proxy company elsewhere? — red flag: none and no other evidence of demand
- Who are the competitors and what do you see that they missed? — red flag: "no competitors" or no insight vs entrenched ones
- Do you want it and do you know people who do? — red flag: no on both
- Does it scale without people? — red flag: services or agency business
- Which idea space are you in and what is its hit rate? — red flag: consumer hardware, social networks, adtech (per the cited decade of YC data, with a caveat)
- Did you pick the first idea, or wait for the perfect one? — red flag: either extreme

## Red flags / kill signals
- Solution in search of a problem — "made-up problem"
- Tarpit idea with no insight into why prior attempts failed
- Idea without founder-market fit
- No competitors — often "no one wants the product"
- Services business pretending to be a startup
- Idea space with low historical hit rate
- Founder neither wants it nor knows people who do
- Huge abstract societal problem as the idea

## Green flags
- Founder-market fit (PlanGrid: construction expert plus developer)
- Acute problem where the alternative is nothing (Brex)
- Recent change creating opportunity (Checkr, Gather Town)
- Proxy company elsewhere (DoorDash for Rappi)
- Existing competitors that most users ignore, plus a specific technical insight (Dropbox)
- Hard, boring or competitive space others avoid (Stripe, Gusto)
- Idea found organically or from unusual vantage point (Vetcove, Rezi, Replit)
- Systematic search with user conversation in a fertile idea space (A to B)

## Named examples
- PlanGrid: founder-market fit (Tracy in construction, Ralph developer)
- Coinbase 2012: small but rapidly growing market
- Brex (W17): credit card for startups; no bank would offer one
- Checkr: background checks API for gig delivery workforce
- Rappi [spelled "rapid" in captions]: DoorDash as proxy for Latin America
- Fivetran: pivoted data analysis tools until finding what companies wanted; good idea space
- Stripe: schlep blindness; Gusto: boring payroll; Dropbox: 20th file-storage company
- Resi: real estate and debt-financing experts, idea space real estate x fintech
- Vetcove: founders' father a veterinarian; Amazon for vets
- DoorDash: wish-it-existed recipe (and tarpit danger)
- Gather Town: pivoted at pandemic start
- Nuvocargo: flexport for Latin America, systematic search
- A to B: fuel cards for truckers, drove to truck stops
- Replit: began as something Amjad found interesting
- Tarpit example: app to coordinate meeting friends

## Tensions
- Says competition is good and a sign of demand, against "avoid crowded markets", but needs "a new insight" against entrenched competitors.
- Says boring beats fun and passion follows success, against "follow your passion" and against founder love-of-problem advice elsewhere (Seibel: lose motivation if you do not care).
- Says brainstormed ideas are weaker (70 percent of top 100 ideas came organically) but then gives seven recipes for generating ideas and a systematic search (Nuvocargo, A to B).
- Idea-space hit rates are time-bound ("spaces flipped from hot to cold").
- Nuance: execution matters as much as the initial idea; the only real test is launching.
