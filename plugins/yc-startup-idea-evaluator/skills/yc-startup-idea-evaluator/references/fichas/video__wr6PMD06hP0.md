## One-line thesis
Cold outbound fails mostly because of targeting and lack of manual learning, not copy: do at least 100 hand-written outreaches, target the person with the exact pain, write short peer-tone messages with one clear call to action, and debug in a fixed order.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Manual before automation**: Zero replies from an unthought campaign teach nothing; messaging, targeting, prospects, deliverability and subject could all be the cause. Check that the founder did the first 100 by hand. Quote: "my recommendation is to do at least 100 outreaches by hand before you automate anything"
- **Targeting beats copy**: The wrong job titles never buy; check who is actually receiving the email. Quote: "those wrong job titles were never going to buy no matter how good your email was"
- **Find the buyer from closed deals**: Look at who pushed the deal, who signed and who paid among current customers and replies. Quote: "who is the person who is pushing the last deal to close who actually signed the contract who is the person who paid"
- **Who has most to gain**: With no customers yet, ask who in the organisation has the most to gain (a CSO might be fired for a breach; an engineering manager would rather not be monitored). Quote: "ask yourself who in the organization has the most to gain from buying your software"
- **Verify company fit**: List tools return false matches (IT consultancies when searching tech companies). Quote: "verify individually that each company is a good fit for your customer or your message is going to fall on deaf ears"
- **Intent signals**: Job postings and company growth tell you who needs the product (compensation planning at about 100 to 500 employees). Quote: "every product has signals like this your job is to discover yours"
- **Start with the warm network**: First customers convert far better from friends, family, investors. Quote: "you really want to shake the tree to make sure you hit everyone you know because the conversion rates will be much much higher"
- **Message about their pain, not your features**: Executives care about quarterly targets, not Rust or fast uploads. Quote: "executives don't care about your product futures they don't care that you have blazing fast uploads they don't care that your database is built in Rust"
- **One-sentence explanation plus credibility**: Name impressive customers or relevant background; keep the product explanation to a single sentence. Quote: "I would try to make it just one sentence you don't want to spend paragraphs and paragraphs of details here"
- **Specific call to action**: Use a concrete ask, not "does this sound interesting". Quote: "Got 20 minutes to hop on a call this week?"
- **Short emails**: Five to eight sentences for email, two to five for LinkedIn. Quote: "five to eight sentences is good for email no more than two to five for LinkedIn"
- **Peer tone and credibility example**: A good email was informal, referenced a prior employer's solution to the same problem, noticed something on the prospect's site and asked for an experiment. Quote: "it's written in a really informal tone that makes him sound like a peer"
- **Weird subject lines win**: "broken agents" outperformed the descriptive "better agents for every workflow". Quote: "creative and kind of weird ones can end up being the winners"
- **Baseline reply rate**: Two to three replies per 100 emails is a place to start iterating. Quote: "even just two to three replies on 100 emails is a great place to start iterating"
- **Debug order**: Right person, right companies, subject lines, messaging, materials (website, LinkedIn), deliverability; if all fine and zero replies, suspect product-market fit. Quote: "this often might be indicative of a deeper product market fit issue"
- **Follow up and breakup email**: Follow up two to four times; the last email gives a low-pressure out and gets higher replies; a real no is useful data. Quote: "reply rates I find are much higher on these breakup emails because it makes them really low pressure for them to say no"
- **Speed on replies**: Time kills deals. Quote: "time kills deals"
- **Block out time**: Founders avoid outbound; schedule it. Quote: "the number one difference between founders who actually succeed without founders who don't is actually following through on the activity that they commit to"
- **Use the customer's words**: Ask what got their attention and what made them buy; reuse that phrasing. Quote: "your customers are always going to write better copy than you do"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- How many outreaches did you write by hand, and what did you change after the first twenty? — Zero or blasted a thousand.
- What job titles felt the pain, and how do you know? — Mixed titles including customer success and DevRel for an infra product.
- What intent signal says a company needs you now? — None identified.
- What is your reply rate and which step in the debug order have you ruled out? — No numbers.
- What did the customer say got their attention? — Never asked.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Automated blast of a thousand emails with zero replies — nothing learned.
- Zero replies after fixing target, subject, message, materials and deliverability — likely a product-market fit problem.
- Email about features or technology rather than the buyer's problem.

## Green flags
- Founder-led sales with hands-on research of each prospect.
- Replies, even few, to iterate on.
- Scheduled daily outbound blocks.
