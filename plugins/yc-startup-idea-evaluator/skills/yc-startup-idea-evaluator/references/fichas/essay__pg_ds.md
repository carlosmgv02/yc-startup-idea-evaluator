## One-line thesis
Startups take off because founders push them: recruit users one by one, delight them far beyond what big companies can, and treat the idea as a pair (what you build plus the unscalable thing you do to get going).

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Founders make it take off**: Do not accept "build it and they will come"; check what the founders will personally do to get the first users. Quote: "Actually startups take off because the founders make them take off."
- **Manual recruiting**: The founders should be able to name how they will go out and get the first users. Quote: "You can't wait for users to come to you. You have to go out and get them."
- **Collison installation**: Good: set the user up on the spot rather than sending a link. Bad: "we'll send you a link". Quote: "Right then, give me your laptop"
- **Weekly growth rate**: Measure progress as weekly growth; 10% a week from a small base compounds. Quote: "After a year you'll have 14,000 users, and after 2 years you'll have 2 million."
- **Small absolute numbers are fine**: Judge by growth rate, not by absolute size. Quote: "The mistake they make is to underestimate the power of compound growth."
- **Ask the right question about larval startups**: Not "is it taking over the world" but the potential if founders do the right things. Quote: "how big could this company get if the founders did the right things?"
- **Find the vein of users**: If you are not your own user, launch untargeted and watch who is most enthusiastic. Quote: "get some initial set of users by doing a comparatively untargeted launch, and then to observe which kind seem most enthusiastic, and seek out more like them."
- **Build for yourself**: Building for your own problem hands you the first market. Quote: "If you build something to solve your own problems, then you only have to find your peers, which is usually straightforward."
- **User experience over product**: Early on, the experience of being the user should be insanely great, not the product. Quote: "you can and should give users an insanely great experience with an early, incomplete, buggy product, if you make up the difference with attentiveness."
- **Delight scales better than expected**: Worry about not scaling service is premature. Quote: "I have never once seen a startup lured down a blind alley by trying too hard to make their initial users happy."
- **Quantum of utility**: Ship when it has minimal utility and watch usage. Quote: "get something in front of users as soon as it has a quantum of utility, and then see what they do with it."
- **Early feedback is the best**: Direct contact with earliest users yields the best feedback. Quote: "The feedback you get from engaging directly with your earliest users will be the best you ever get."
- **Contained fire**: Find a narrow subset where critical mass is quick. Quote: "It's always worth asking if there's a subset of the market in which you can get a critical mass of users quickly."
- **Quickest to sign up over highest payers**: When choosing the subset. Quote: "it's usually best to pick the former, because those are probably the early adopters."
- **Startups as early adopters**: For B2B, other startups are the best first customers. Quote: "Among companies, the best early adopters are usually other startups."
- **Consult for one user**: B2B: pick a single user and fit their needs perfectly. Quote: "As long as you can find just one user who really needs something and can act on that need, you've got a toehold in making something people want"
- **Do not charge for the over-attentiveness**: Consulting is safe only if unpaid. Quote: "when they start paying you by the hour — they expect you to do everything."
- **Be the software**: Do by hand what you plan to automate. Quote: "you'll know exactly what to build because you'll have muscle memory from doing it yourself."
- **Manual before automatic**: Solving manually beats automating a non-solution. Quote: "less frightening than the far more common case of having something automatic that doesn't yet solve anyone's problems."
- **Big launches do not matter**: Launch only needs a core of users. Quote: "How well you're doing a few months later will depend more on how happy you made those users than how many there were of them."
- **Partnerships are suspect**: A big-company partnership is not a growth engine. Quote: "that was way more work than we expected, and we ended up getting practically nothing out of it."
- **Effort test**: Any strategy omitting founder effort is suspect. Quote: "Any strategy that omits the effort — whether it's expecting a big launch to get you users, or a big partner — is ipso facto suspect."
- **Idea as a vector**: Evaluate the idea as product plus unscalable starting move. Quote: "thinking of them as pairs of what you're going to build, plus the unscalable thing(s) you're going to do initially to get the company going."
- **Sales is the founder's job**: Cannot be outsourced initially. Quote: "you can't avoid doing sales by hiring someone to do it for you. You have to do sales yourself initially."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is the unscalable thing you will do in the first months to get users? — nothing, or "launch and see".
- Can you name your first 10 users and what you will do for each one? — vague "marketing channel" answers.
- What is your weekly growth rate? — no number, or no one measuring.
- Which subset of the market would feel the product is truly for them? — "all businesses".
- Who is most enthusiastic among early users, and where do you find more like them? — no observation of the early users.
- Which parts are you doing by hand today? — everything is automated but nobody uses it.
- Who does the sales and how much time do they spend on it? — the CEO delegates sales to a hire.
- Are your friends early adopters of this product? — corporate-world friends, no natural first market.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Plan relies on a Big Launch (embargoed, many publications) — "Think of some successful startups. How many of their launches do you remember?"
- Plan relies on a partnership with a big company as the break — "they don't work for startups in general".
- Founders won't do sales or talk to strangers — "shyness and laziness".
- Imitating big companies' flaws such as indifference to individual users (Garry Tan note).
- No way to find users to recruit manually — "is probably a bad idea, at least for those founders."
- Perfectionism before putting it in front of users — "Perfectionism is often an excuse for procrastination".
- Enterprise idea with no easy small set of users and no connections — "should you even be working on such an idea?"
- Founders dismissing their own startup — "The big danger is that you'll dismiss your startup yourself."

## Green flags
- Founders recruit users in person, door to door, set them up on the spot.
- Growth measured weekly, around 10% a week.
- Hand-written thank you notes and extraordinary attention to early users.
- Narrow initial market where users feel it is theirs.
- Founders solve the problem by hand behind a software front.
- Founders use the software on behalf of lukewarm users.
- Early users are other startups or the founders' own peers.

## Named examples
- Stripe: Collison installation; manual merchant account signups behind "instant" accounts; aggressive early acquisition.
- Airbnb: door to door in New York, helping hosts improve listings; "about 30 days" of engagement made the difference.
- Facebook: Harvard first, then specific colleges, building course lists per school.
- Wufoo: hand-written thank you notes.
- Pinterest, Ben Silbermann: found design interest among early users and recruited at a design bloggers conference.
- Meraki and Pebble: assembling hardware yourself; Pebble sold "$10 million worth of watches" on Kickstarter after hand-assembling the first several hundred.
- Viaweb: used its own software to build stores for lukewarm merchants.
- Microsoft: Basic interpreters for a few thousand hobbyists.
- Segway, Google Wave: famous launch flops; Wave possibly killed partly by its overdone launch.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "build a better mousetrap and they will come" and the idea that no uptake means no market.
- Nuances "scale from day one": engineers' instinct to avoid handholding is wrong early.
- Hardware and B2B variants (pull a Meraki, consult for one user) differ from software consumer advice.
- Choose fastest-to-sign-up segment over highest-paying (note 8), which can contradict revenue-first advice.
- "Don't be paid by the hour" nuances consulting-to-product paths.
