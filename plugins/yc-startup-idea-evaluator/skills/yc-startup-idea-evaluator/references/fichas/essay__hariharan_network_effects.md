## One-line thesis
Growth and virality are not network effects; a real network effect shows up as improving engagement and retention in older cohorts plus high barriers to exit for the user, and the right questions differ for a network, a marketplace and a platform.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Growth is not network effect**: fast growth can come from a superior product; check value, not speed. Virality is speed of growth, network effect is value. Quote: "virality is actually about the speed of growth. It’s not about the value."
- **Retention is the test**: if DAU/MAU or cohort usage improves as users are added, there is a network effect; flat or decaying cohorts means growth only. Quote: "that percentage kept improving and so that retention is the sign of network effect."
- **Engagement as the strongest signal**: older cohorts must use the product more over time, otherwise stop and ask why. Quote: "retention of your users, you’ll automatically know whether it’s a network or not. It is the single strongest sign of a network effect."
- **Two-part test: entry and exit**: barriers to entry for competitors AND barriers to exit for users; founders usually miss the second. Quote: "And barriers to exit for the user. I think the second one is the one people usually miss out on."
- **Switching cost via accumulated value**: strong when the user has connections, history and setup inside the product (Facebook, WhatsApp); weak when the product is a point-to-point service with no value trapped (ride sharing). Quote: "beyond taking you from point A to point B, I don’t have any other value trapped in it."
- **Destination app signal**: rising time spent per user, with users going to the app directly, indicates rising barriers to exit. Quote: "is your app a destination app? And that’s a sign for barriers to exit of for user."
- **Classify yourself first**: network, marketplace or platform; the questions differ. Quote: "are you a network, or a marketplace, or a platform? And I think it’s important to understand these three."
- **Network checklist**: entry strategy, then growth to critical mass (the only moat), then an engagement trigger, then engagement of old cohorts, then which cluster to start in. Quote: "If you have a product with early signs of network effect, the only way you build a moat is by growing because you have to get that critical mass."
- **Engagement trigger (aha metric)**: find the number of connections/actions within N days that predicts retention. Quote: "if I could connect you to 10 friends within 14 days, the retention was much higher."
- **Cluster entry**: start dense in one cluster and measure before expanding (Facebook at Harvard: at least 80% of students on it, 60-70% daily). Quote: "where do I start? So which cluster do I start, or are there some kind of topology which you could sort of attack first."
- **Do not out-grow your engagement**: growing too fast can lose the older base and push you to growth-only features. Quote: "you could end up growing too quickly and lose your older user base"
- **Low-frequency products**: measure rebookings cohort by cohort (Airbnb). Quote: "the way to measure that is bookings and rebookings cohort after cohort."
- **Marketplace: demand first**: scaled marketplaces nearly always focus on demand first; supply follows demand. Quote: "usually in marketplaces, supply always goes to where demand is. So if there’s enough demand, they’ll be more people who sign up on the supply side."
- **Marketplace balance**: oversupplying one side degrades the experience; measure the balance and track failed fulfilment (Instacart tracked lost deliveries). Quote: "If you scale one side too much, they’re too fast, then the other side suffers."
- **City-by-city playbook**: perfect one city, then use it to roll out faster; staff supply on standby based on expected demand. Quote: "for them to get their playbook right in San Francisco before they sort of rolled it out to another city."
- **Marketplaces are slow**: expect a long time to critical mass. Quote: "Airbnb took 36 months or more, it was a very slow growth."
- **Money side vs subsidy side**: identify the price-sensitive, harder side and subsidize it if the other side will pay. Quote: "try and subsidize the harder side if you know you can drive a lot value for the money side."
- **Local on-demand = weak network effect**: highly local networks are hard to replicate nationally; do not assume social-network-grade moat. Quote: "On Demand has been the biggest debate. I think because it’s highly local."
- **Winner-take-all without network effects**: supply-side economies of scale plus operational excellence can also give monopolistic share. Quote: "companies with supplies at economies of scale, especially which have huge operational challenges than who execute really well can have monopolistic share too."
## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Are you a network, a marketplace or a platform, and what is your entry cluster? — cannot name a dense starting cluster, "everyone" is the market.
- Do older cohorts use the product more than newer ones? — flat or declining cohort engagement means growth, not network effect.
- What is your engagement trigger (X actions in Y days that predicts retention)? — no data on what creates the aha moment.
- If an equally good competitor appeared, why would a user not switch? — no value trapped in the product.
- Which side do you launch first, and what is your lost-fulfilment rate in a new market? — supply-first plan with no demand proof.
- Which side is the money side and which the subsidy side? — both sides priced the same without reasoning.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Citing fast user growth as proof of network effects — virality and word of mouth are unpaid growth, not value to the network.
- Point-to-point service with no stored value for the user — low barrier to exit, only a weak local network effect.
- Features added only to drive growth while engagement of old users stalls — per the source, check why older cohorts are not using it more.
- Launching as a feature inside a platform you do not control, with no distribution plan — hard to find distribution against Facebook, Twitter, Snapchat.

## Green flags
- Retention or DAU/MAU improving as the network grows.
- Users spend more time and go directly to the app (destination app).
- Users have connections, history and communication stored in the product.
- Dense initial cluster with measured penetration before expansion.
- In marketplaces, customers repeat orders and supply arrives on its own where demand is.
- Existing users use the product for more use cases over time (WhatsApp from SMS to photos and video).

## Named examples
- Facebook: clustered launch at Harvard, 80% of students; 10 friends in 14 days trigger; Google+ failed to copy the graph.
- Airbnb: word-of-mouth growth, not viral; marketplace that took 36 months or more; measured by rebookings.
- Instacart: demand first; changed from grocery aggregator to named retail stores within six months; city-by-city rollout; tracked lost deliveries.
- Uber and Lyft: weak, city-level network effect plus supply-side economies of scale (trips per hour lowers cost).
- WhatsApp: moved from status feature to messaging within about six months; phone number and address book instead of usernames.
- musical.ly: pivot from education app; used Instagram and Twitter as distribution, then built community in-app.
- Adobe PDF: free reader as subsidy side, paid writer as money side.
- Slack: network needing one team member to create the aha moment.
- Amazon first party: scale economies without network effects.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "growth equals moat": growth only builds a moat if engagement and retention improve with it.
- Nuances the "marketplaces are network effect businesses" idea: local on-demand marketplaces may have only weak network effects, but supply-side scale can still win.
- Winner-take-all is stated for network-effect businesses, but she also says strong networks end up with "a few players, rather than one."
