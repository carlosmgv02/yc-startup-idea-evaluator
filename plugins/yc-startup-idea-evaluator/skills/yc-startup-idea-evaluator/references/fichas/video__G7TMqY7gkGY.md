## One-line thesis
Rookie mistakes from YC founders: building many use cases before nailing one, copying a model from another market assuming fit, treating investor or press validation as proof, and crowdfunding hardware too early; the cure is a simple product that works plus ground-truth answers from customers.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **One feature that works**: Quote: "a product that doesn't work with lots of features is infinitely worse than a product with one feature that works"
- **Nail a single use case**: Quote: "we tried to accommodate more than one use case in our product before nailing a single use case and proving out that they even existed business serving that customer"
- **Boiling the ocean**: Quote: "your plan to start the elaborate plan is to build it all at once before getting a single user"
- **The founder's useful lie**: Quote: "every founder starts their company with the lie in their head that they know about what their customer wants and they know how to solve the problem"
- **Build less before user contact**: Quote: "the more you build before putting your product in front of a user the more you just let that lie go wild"
- **Googleitis**: Quote: "people looking at companies that they use today and not understanding what they looked like at the beginning"
- **Start simple, grind**: OpenSea spent 1-2 years on a barely working simple site, shipping once a day or week. Quote: "they started simple and they made the simple thing work and now they're adding more complexity to it"
- **Copy-from-abroad trap**: Quote: "if blank works here it should work there"
- **Validation is not fit**: investors, press, friends, competitions. Quote: "you can get validation from all those things and it could still not work"
- **Ground truth**: Quote: "you really can't substitute anything for ground truth answers"
- **Observe the problem yourself**: Quote: "you actually have to go observe the problem talk to the people who have the problem try to figure out what's going on and iterate"
- **Authority figures lack answers**: Quote: "if you're looking for authority figures to tell you your idea is good you still aren't really getting the point of these videos"
- **Revenue as core stat**: Quote: "let's look at a core stat right like you charge people what's your revenue look like"
- **Growth rationalization**: Quote: "we have to increase the number of customers who could buy us before we increase the customers who do buy us"
- **Runway discipline**: Quote: "when the runway gets down to about 12 months the magical thinking starts fading"
- **Discipline over genius**: Quote: "for every ounce of discipline you have you can probably lose five ten ounces of intelligence and still outperform you know"
- **Focused work**: Quote: "the folks that are really good at this are just better at doing focused work"
- **Hardware crowdfunding**: Quote: "usually do it after you've shipped and manufactured a couple of versions already"
- **Crowdfunding unknowns**: Quote: "no idea how to build it no idea how much how much building it would cost no no idea how much the thing should cost"
- **Taking money from strangers**: Quote: "if you take money from people at scale yeah on the internet they rightfully will hold you accountable"
- **Software is easier**: Quote: "there is one better strategy do a software company a lot easier"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Which single use case and customer have you nailed? — several use cases, none proven.
- Have you got a single user yet? — building the full elaborate product first.
- What is your revenue? — "not really focused on revenue right now, we want to grow".
- Has a real user in this country told you they want it, or is it "worked in country x so it works here"? — fit assumed from another market.
- What validation do you have that is not from investors, press or friends? — only that.
- What do your own customer conversations show vs what a mentor says? — asking for advice from authorities.
- Do you know how to build it, what it costs and what to charge? (hardware or crowdfunding) — no idea on any.
- Where does your calendar go? — conferences, press, hiring instead of focused work.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Boiling the ocean: many features and use cases at once, features that do not reliably work.
- Cloning a model from another market and hiring fast before checking users want it.
- Treating funding or press as proof.
- Raising lots of money, then "come back when it's not working" with a team of 50 and no revenue.
- Early hardware crowdfunding with unknown cost, price and timeline.
- Looking for a teacher or authority instead of the customer.

## Green flags
- Simple v1 that works reliably (Zen Payroll: payroll only).
- Shipping daily or weekly.
- Disciplined, focused work with no distractions.
- Founder doing ground-truth research with customers.

## Named examples
- Amazon: began as a simple site selling books.
- OpenSea (YC 2018): pivoted, 1 to 2 years grinding a basic NFT buy/sell site, no own wallet.
- Gusto (Zen Payroll, W12): started with only online payroll.
- Teespring: YC company for ordering T-shirts in quantity (did not work out).
- Hardware Kickstarter companies: cooler-with-blender example of untested plans and angry backers.

## Tensions
- Investors like funding "works there so works here" ideas, which then fail after funding.
- Crowdfunding was viewed as a panacea six or seven years earlier; now it is only a tool, best used with v2/v3.
- Genius is mistaken for discipline.
- Hard workers are dangerous; lazy competitors are founders' friends.
- Admits advice-seeking is itself the mistake: authority figures "don't have the answers".
