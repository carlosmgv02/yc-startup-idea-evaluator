## One-line thesis
Founders must talk directly to users with specific, past-behavior questions (not pitches or hypotheticals) to validate the problem, pick the best first customer and measure progress to product-market fit.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Founders own the user connection**: Quote: "the best companies are the ones where the founders themselves maintain a direct connection to their users"
- **Only two jobs**: Quote: "there are only two things that you must do in order to search your company you need to code or build your product and talk to users"
- **Do not pitch in interviews**: the goal is data, not selling. Quote: "it is not to sell them on using your product"
- **No hypotheticals**: "would you use / pay for this feature" is wrong. Quote: "instead talk about specifics that have already occurred in the users life"
- **Listen, do not talk**: Quote: "you're bringing hard data real facts about users lives to the table"
- **Q1 hardest part**: Quote: "what is the hardest part about doing the thing that you're trying to solve"
- **Frequency or pain**: Quote: "the best startups are looking for problems that people face on a regular basis or that they're painful enough to warrant solving"
- **Q2 last time**: Quote: "tell me about the last time that you encountered this problem"
- **Q3 why was it hard**: answers inform marketing. Quote: "customers don't buy what they don't buy the what they buy the why"
- **Q4 existing solutions as burning-problem test**: Quote: "if potential customers are not already exploring potential solutions to their problem it's possible that the problem that you're trying to solve is not a burning enough problem"
- **Q4 also maps competition**: Quote: "what are the other competition out there what will your product be compared against"
- **Q5 what do you not love about what you tried**: Quote: "this is the beginning of your potential feature set"
- **Do not ask for features**: Quote: "users in general are not great at identifying the the next features that they want in the product"
- **First users start with yourself and friends**: Quote: "some of the best companies are products or services that are built for the founders themself"
- **Few interviews are enough to start**: Quote: "every good user kind of research strategy begins with just one or two people"
- **Show up in person**: firefighters example. Quote: "they managed to get dozens of in-person no 10 to 15-minute long meetings just by showing up"
- **Wrong first customer is a trap**: Quote: "if you choose the wrong first customer that you may be led down a path that that constrains you or artificially traps you without actually getting paid by that first"
- **Three numbers for the best first customer**: Quote: "how much does this problem cost them today and I like to get a hard number"
- **Frequency**: Quote: "the best problems that startups can can target are ones that are encountered more frequently"
- **Budget and authority**: Quote: "they just don't have the budget they don't have the authority to actually solve the problem that's their boss"
- **Stack-rank in a spreadsheet**: Quote: "you actually do like a simple stack rank that just pulls the best answers to those questions up to the top"
- **PMF as a measurable survey**: ask "how would you feel if you could no longer use" the product. Quote: "how would you feel if you could no longer use superhuman three answers very disappointed somewhat disappointed not disappointed"
- **40 percent threshold**: Quote: "a 40 percent or more of your user base reports that they would be very disappointed if your product went away"
- **Pay-first feature test**: Quote: "here's an upgrade flow if you want this new product put your credit card or if you want this new feature put your credit card information or pay more even"
- **Discard compliments and fluff**: Quote: "they actually are not useful information because it's not specific it's more of a general statement about your product"
- **Phone number at signup**: Quote: "put contact information including phone number which is like a direct connection to customers pretty high up in the user signup flow"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- When did you last talk to a user, and what did they tell you about a specific past incident? — red flag: outsourced to sales or product hires; only hypotheticals
- What have your users already tried to solve this? — red flag: nothing; the problem may not be burning
- How much does the problem cost them today, how often does it occur and who holds the budget? — red flag: no numbers, or the contact has no authority
- Who is your best first customer and why on those three axes? — red flag: first customer chosen by friendliness or brand
- What share of users would be "very disappointed" without the product? — red flag: under 40 percent or unmeasured
- Did anyone pay or enter a card before the feature existed? — red flag: only verbal interest
- Were the interviews unbiased, or did you pitch? — red flag: feedback is compliments

## Red flags / kill signals
- Hiring a go-between (Office Space analogy) — founders lose the direct connection
- Interviews that are pitches — founders talk instead of listening
- Hypothetical questions about features — "if we built this feature would you be interested"
- Users not already trying to solve the problem — not burning
- Compliments and hypotheticals treated as data
- Operator-level contacts without budget

## Green flags
- Founder is the user
- Detailed notes from the first 5 to 10 interviews
- Problem is frequent (daily use like a watch)
- First customer has high cost of problem, high frequency and large budget
- 40 percent or more "very disappointed" (superhuman metric)
- Showing up in person and getting dozens of meetings

## Named examples
- Pebble (Migicovsky): CES guerrilla meetings at $0; daily-use device gave feedback signal
- Dropbox/Drew at MIT: worked example of five questions
- The Mom Test (book): three errors (talk about idea, hypotheticals, talk too much)
- Firefighters company (YC batch): showed up at fire stations after cold email failed
- Smoothie blender example: McDonald's beats French Laundry and Google cafe on cost, frequency, budget
- Superhuman CEO: weekly "very disappointed" survey, 40 percent bar
- Paul Graham and Marc Andreessen: PMF definitions called vague and retroactive

## Tensions
- Contradicts "sell your idea to everyone": interviews are for data. Also counter to "build what users ask for" (Ford "faster horse" point).
- Pay-before-build test favors cash behavior over stated intent.
- Warns early user interviews with friends and moms are weak ("The mom test"), yet recommends starting with friends and coworkers for warm intros.
- PMF definitions from PG and Andreessen are called not actionable; replaced by a 40 percent survey proxy.
