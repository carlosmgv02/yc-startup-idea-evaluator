## One-line thesis
Startups are counterintuitive: what wins is expertise on your users and a real problem (not startup mechanics or tricks), and the best ideas come from living at the leading edge of something you are curious about, not from trying to think of startup ideas.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Expert on users, not on startups**: check whether founders know their users and problem deeply, not fundraising mechanics. Quote: "The way to succeed in a startup is not to be an expert on startups, but to be an expert on your users and the problem you're solving for them."
- **Playing house detector**: a team with a plausible idea, money raised, a cool office and hires but no product people want is imitating the form. Quote: "while imitating all the outward forms of a startup they have neglected the one thing that's actually essential: making something people want."
- **Looking for the trick**: founders asking "how do we raise / how do we grow" want a hack; good answer is usually to make something people want. Quote: "So many of the conversations YC partners have with young founders begin with the founder asking "How do we..." and the partner replying "Just...""
- **Users cannot be gamed**: no boss to trick, only users. Test: does usage grow because the product does what users want? Quote: "There is no boss to trick, only users, and all users care about is whether your product does what they want."
- **Usage graph beats fundraising skill**: a flat usage graph with perfect fundraising technique is worse than loved product with none. Quote: "A founder who knows nothing about fundraising but has made something users love will have an easier time raising money than one who knows every trick in the book but"
- **Fundraising is not success**: do not treat a round as the measure. Quote: "Since fundraising appears to be the measure of success for startups (another classic noob mistake)"
- **Faked investors are a trap**: sounding convincing may get 1-2 rounds but the company is doomed. Quote: "you can fool investors for at least one and perhaps even two rounds of funding. But it's not in your interest to. The company is ultimately doomed."
- **Trust your gut about people**: misgivings about a cofounder, employee, investor or acquirer are a signal; do not override with "impressive". Quote: "If you're thinking about getting involved with someone — as a cofounder, an employee, an investor, or an acquirer — and you have misgivings about them, trust your gut."
- **Cofounders you know**: Quote: "Work with people you genuinely like, and you've known long enough to be sure."
- **Coachability**: founders who listen more tend to succeed. Quote: "Some founders listen more than others, and this tends to be a predictor of success."
- **Idea by osmosis, not by brainstorm**: a deliberately brainstormed idea is bad and plausible-sounding, so costly to disprove. Quote: "if you make a conscious effort to think of startup ideas, the ideas you come up with will not merely be bad, but bad and plausible-sounding, meaning you'll waste a"
- **Side-project origin**: best startups often began as side projects not meant to be companies. Quote: "The best startups almost have to start as side projects, because great ideas tend to be such outliers that your conscious mind would reject them as ideas for companies."
- **Leading edge of technology**: check whether the founders live in the future of some technology, so the idea feels obvious to them. Quote: "to get yourself to the leading edge of some technology — to cause yourself, as Paul Buchheit put it, to "live in the future.""
- **Need it yourself**: example of builder scratching own itch (VoIP at Harvard). Quote: "He just wanted to talk to his girlfriend in Taiwan without paying for long distance calls"
- **Domain expertise is what matters**: Quote: "The component of entrepreneurship that really matters is domain expertise."
- **Real problems are interesting**: heuristic for taste: boring known ideas are intolerable. Quote: "whether you find known boring ideas intolerable. Could you endure studying literary theory, or working in middle management at a large company?"
- **Founder toughness is not predictable**: initial swagger or doubt do not predict outcomes; do not over-index on pitch demeanor. Quote: "there is little correlation between founders' initial attitudes and how well their companies do."
- **Intelligence is not the gap**: smartness is easy to assess; the unpredictable part is toughness and ambition. Quote: "It was easy to tell how smart they were, and most people reading this will be over that threshold. The hard part was predicting how tough and ambitious they would"
- **All-consuming commitment**: check the founders know it never gets easier. Quote: "in every single case the founders say the same thing. It never gets any easier."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What do you know about your users that nobody else does? — red flag: knowledge is about startups/funding, not users
- Where did this idea come from? — red flag: brainstormed to fit a startup template, no personal contact with the problem
- Why do you want investors to like you before users do? — red flag: pitch focuses on tricks and valuation
- How is usage growing? — red flag: flat graph, or no graph
- Why these cofounders, and how long have you known them? — red flag: impressive people the founder has personal misgivings about
- What advice did you disagree with and why? — red flag: dismisses all outside advice (founders "ignore" counterintuitive advice)
- What is your relationship with the technology's leading edge? — red flag: idea not tied to any frontier or expertise

## Red flags / kill signals
- "Playing house": office, hires, round, no product people want — neglects making something people want
- Trick-seeking questions instead of product questions — starting a startup is where gaming the system stops working
- Founder knows all fundraising terms but has no usage — alarms, not advantage
- Idea chosen because it sounds like a startup — plausible-sounding but bad
- Founders with personal misgivings about a partner but ignoring them because the person is impressive
- Fundraising used as scoreboard

## Green flags
- Obsessive knowledge of users and their problem
- Idea emerged from the founder's own project or need
- Founders who listen intently (Airbnb example)
- Domain expertise built out of genuine curiosity
- Usage growth driving the fundraising story

## Named examples
- Mark Zuckerberg: succeeded "despite being a complete noob at startups" because he understood users
- Airbnb founders (Brian Chesky, Joe Gebbia): listened intently during YC; good at design and organizing groups, not just technology
- Larry Page: expert on search, driven by curiosity; also an example of all-consuming CEO life
- Apple, Yahoo, Google, Facebook: started as side projects, not meant to be companies
- Harvard grad student with voice-over-IP: built it to call his girlfriend, no startup intent

## Tensions
- Contradicts the common advice to study entrepreneurship or startup mechanics: says that knowledge is unnecessary and possibly dangerous.
- Says trust instincts about people but not instincts about startups.
- Advice aimed at college students: do not start at 20; most advice elsewhere (including YC) encourages starting young. Note [7]: "it's gratuitously stupid to do that at 20" unless a side project truly takes off.
- Against "find an idea by brainstorming" frameworks often used in idea evaluation.
