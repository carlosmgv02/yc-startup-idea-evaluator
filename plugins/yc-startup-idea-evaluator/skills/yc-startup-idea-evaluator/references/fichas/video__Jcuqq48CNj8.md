## One-line thesis
Airbnb, Coinbase and Stripe each had a founder with the problem, a bad dominant incumbent, a 10x better product in reach, near-universal skepticism from founders and investors, and a market bigger than the founders knew: be contrarian where the critics are not users.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Three common themes**: timing/pre-existing product, most people think it is horrible, bigger than the founders knew. Quote: "each of these opportunities turned out to be bigger than the founders even knew when they started"
- **10x better than existing**: Quote: "there was an opportunity to make something that was 10x better than what existed"
- **Do not care about incumbents if users hate them**: Quote: "if you're a user of that product or your friends or users of that product and they all hate it"
- **Why existing products were bad**: diagnose specific failures. Quote: "there were two fundamental facts that made the existing products not good"
- **Incumbent charging the wrong side**: Quote: "hosts are the game if you can get the most hosts you win you have the best inventory but if you charge them upfront to be on your site you're probably doing it wrong" [sic: "up run"]
- **Own problem**: Quote: "they were solving their own problem"
- **Use your own product**: Airbnb discovered the payments need when its founder forgot money and was thought a fraud. Quote: "when he used his own product and he forgot to bring money for payment and the host thought he was a fraud he realized oh crap like our site needs to allow people to pay"
- **Insights can come after starting**: Quote: "you don't have to have all the valuable insights before you start"
- **Peers' pain**: Quote: "if all of your smart technical friends are complaining about the same thing maybe it's a real problem"
- **Simple promise suffices**: Coinbase was a site to buy and sell bitcoin. Quote: "that was enough of an insight to be the basis for like a hundred billion dollar company"
- **Contrarian screen**: the critics' relevance depends on their being users. Quote: "especially you shouldn't care if they're not going to be users if they have no experience with the pain of using the existing product"
- **Developer-as-customer GTM**: Stripe built for programmers (docs, website, beta invites). Quote: "what would a programmer want"
- **Price as insight proof**: Quote: "it was the most expensive product in the market like to make sure people really wanted it"
- **Market math caution**: Quote: "be very careful about pretending that you know how big the market could be"
- **Execution is the hard part**: Quote: "talk is cheap execution's hard execution's expensive"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What did people use before and why was it bad in specific, fixable ways? — "no competitors" or incumbents that are fine.
- Is your product 10x better on some axis? — marginal improvement.
- Have you or your friends suffered this pain firsthand? — no.
- Who told you it is a bad idea, and are they users of the existing product? — dismissal by non-users should be ignored; dismissal by users is a red flag.
- Why now: what changed in timing? — no timing argument.
- Which single simple promise does v1 deliver? — a bundle of features beyond the core.
- Who is the real buyer (programmer vs business)? — building for the wrong customer type.
- What if the market is bigger than your slide says? — no sense of expansion.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Users of the incumbent are satisfied; no 10x gap.
- Being discouraged by a "tiny market" verdict, which would have killed all three.
- Taking investor rejection as a signal to quit (Coinbase had "an impossible time raising money on demo day").
- Charging the side of the market that is the scarce resource upfront (hosts at VRBO).
- Rooting the plan in whiteboard strategy instead of doing.

## Green flags
- Founders with the problem and experience.
- Incumbent product painful to use ("using couchsurfing was so painful").
- Pre-existing dominant competitor with a clear 10x opening.
- Consensus that the idea is bad among non-users.
- Customers eager to pay a premium.

## Named examples
- Airbnb: vs VRBO/Craigslist/Couchsurfing; payments facilitation; hosts not charged; founders needed rent money.
- Coinbase: buying bitcoin pre-2012 was painful (Mt. Gox, money orders); simple buy/sell website; first idea was P2P transfers.
- Stripe: merchant account was "like applying for a mortgage"; 30-page authorize.net contract; developer-first brand; most expensive product; bank deal and PayPal as skepticism.
- Twitch: grew when it added direct payments.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "build the network first, charge later" advice from founders and investors of the time.
- YC's reputation then was funding "third-rate knock-off" companies, yet these were the best.
- Existing competitor is not a reason to avoid an idea, in contrast with "new beats derivative" advice elsewhere.
- Dismissed by experts is a positive signal only if the experts are not users.
