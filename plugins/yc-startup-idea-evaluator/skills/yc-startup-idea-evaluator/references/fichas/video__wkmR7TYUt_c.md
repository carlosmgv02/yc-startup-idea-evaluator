## One-line thesis
Instacart came after about 20 failed products and a lawyer social network built without knowing lawyers; the winner was a pain the founder felt himself, validated by manual fulfilment, then scaled city by city.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Founder-problem fit**: do not start a company to start a company; solve a problem you care about. Quote: "the reason to start a company should never be to start a company. The reason to start a company should be to solve a problem that you truly truly care about."
- **Know the user**: red flag if the founders have never worked with the customer. Quote: "we were building a product for people that we didn't really know much about solving a problem that we didn't know they had."
- **Friends use it without being forced**: the signal missing from every earlier product. Quote: "my friends were using Instacart as well. I didn't have to force them to use it."
- **Be your own first customer, do the work by hand**: the founder shopped and delivered the first orders himself. Quote: "I would drop everything that I was doing and and fulfill the order myself."
- **Unscalable first, scale after demand**: Quote: "once your product has demand, you can figure out how to scale your product."
- **Fast feedback loop with a reachable first segment**: early customers were YC founders, so he called after each order. Quote: "getting getting feedback was very easy. Every time they would just place an order, I would give them a call"
- **Supply-side labor of love for catalog**: no API for Trader Joe's items, so the team bought every item and photographed it. Quote: "the only way we could actually get all the item information was to buy one of every single item at Trader Joe's"
- **Product ambiguity breaks fulfilment**: a bushel photo for "10 bananas" caused a failed delivery; the item listing is a contract between customer and shopper. Quote: "the picture on Instacart for that for that item was a bushel of bananas."
- **Prove the playbook in one city, then expand and measure**: expansion accelerated across cities. Quote: "in 3 weeks we were doing more deliveries than we had done in 33 weeks in the Bay Area."
- **Growth rate**: Quote: "our revenue is growing by 10% week over week and has been growing like that for 20 weeks so far."
- **Asset-light marketplace framing**: software company, no warehouses, trucks or inventory, crowdsourced shoppers. Quote: "we don't actually have any warehouses, no trucks, and we don't hold any inventory."
- **Resilience through failed prototypes**: Quote: "You have to go from failure to failure without losing any steam."
- **Scaling is a hard operations problem after traction**: variable order sizes, shopper speeds, store inventory. Quote: "This is a very very difficult computer science and operations research problem."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Do you feel this pain yourself, and when did you last do the job by hand? — chose the idea to be a founder.
- Do your friends or early users use it without you pushing? — only usage you forced.
- What does fulfilment look like per order, and what breaks it? — never executed one.
- Why did earlier grocery delivery companies fail and what is different? — investors cited WebVan and Cosmo failures; no answer means no thesis.
- Which unscalable thing will you do until there is demand? — building scale first.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Social network for a profession the founders never worked with — "Lawyers don't like technology. Lawyers don't like sharing."
- Switching ideas weekly with no traction signal (analytics for advertisers, Groupon for food) — about 20 failed products.
- Category with famous failures (WebVan, Cosmo): investors were reluctant; the idea needs a reason it differs.

## Green flags
- Organic usage by friends without effort.
- Founder-level customer feedback loop in days.
- Fast city rollouts: Chicago, then Boston, then DC each growing faster than the previous.
- Customers who return and order repeatedly (many early customers were YC founders).

## Named examples
- Instacart: first order June 2, 2012; seed raised despite WebVan history; 10 cities, 10% week-over-week for 20 weeks; $44 million round, over $55 million raised.
- Trader Joe's catalog by buying one of every item; Whole Foods, Costco, Safeway as stores.
- Gary Tan beer order that got him into YC after the deadline had passed.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the idea that "any idea is fine if you hustle": founder-problem fit matters more than persistence alone.
- Founder was flexible about verticals and still failed repeatedly; what worked was personal pain, not openness.
- Marketplace supply (shoppers) was handled by founder doing deliveries; the talk says little about supply liquidity metrics.
