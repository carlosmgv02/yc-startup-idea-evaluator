## One-line thesis
Great startup ideas lie unexploited because founders unconsciously avoid tedious, unpleasant work (schleps); ambitious ideas that involve painful schleps face less competition.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Schleps are the business**: a company that avoids all unpleasant work is not a business plan. Quote: "schleps are not merely inevitable, but pretty much what business consists of. A company is defined by the schleps it will undertake."
- **Just writing code is not a plan**: if the plan is software on a server and money rolling in, with no users, partners or messy integrations, be skeptical. Quote: "No, you can't start a startup by just writing code."
- **Schlep as moat**: an idea that scares most founders has less competition. Quote: "If you pick an ambitious idea, you'll have less competition, because everyone else will have been frightened off by the challenges involved."
- **Undervalued-stock framing**: scariness is a feature of the opportunity. Quote: "they're like undervalued stocks in the sense that there's less demand for them among founders."
- **Known pain, unbuilt solution**: if thousands know a painful problem and nobody works on it, ask what the schlep is that stops them. Quote: "Thousands of people must have known about this problem. And yet when they started startups, they decided to build recipe sites, or aggregators for local events."
- **Importance of the problem**: prefer fixing core infrastructure over problems few care about. Quote: "Why work on problems few care much about and no one will pay for, when you could fix one of the most important components of the world's infrastructure?"
- **Enumerate the schleps**: for an ambitious idea, list the hard parts (deals with incumbents, fraud and security, regulation) and check the team will take them on. Quote: "You'd have to make deals with banks. How do you do that? Plus you're moving money, so you're going to have to deal with fraud, and people trying to break"
- **Take yourself out of the picture**: generate ideas by asking what you wish someone else would solve. Quote: "Instead of asking "what problem should I solve?" ask "what problem do I wish someone else would solve for me?""
- **Willingness to jump in**: Quote: "you should never shrink from it if it's on the path to something great."
- **Ignorance as antidote**: founders who would have quit had they known the obstacles are common among the winners. Quote: "Most successful founders would probably say that if they'd known when they were starting their company about the obstacles they'd have to overcome, they might never have started it."
- **Founders grow with the problem**: a team's current capacity is not the ceiling, though nobody can foresee that. Quote: "In practice the founders grow with the problems. But no one seems able to foresee that, not even older, more experienced founders."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is the most unpleasant part of this business and who does it? — red flag: nobody; plan assumes no schleps
- Why hasn't anyone built this if the pain is so well known? — red flag: no answer, or the answer is that nobody thought of it (rarely true for known pain)
- What problem do you wish someone else would solve for you? — red flag: idea picked only because it is easy to build
- Which parts would you have to do by hand, talk to users about, or negotiate? — red flag: avoids talking to users or other companies
- Are you choosing this idea because it is easy or because it matters? — red flag: recipe-site type idea with low stakes

## Red flags / kill signals
- Plan relies on code alone and no human contact — "Maybe that's possible, but I haven't seen it."
- Idea chosen because it avoids regulation, banks, fraud, partners — unconscious avoidance
- Founder chooses a trivial problem despite knowing a major one — schlep blindness
- Founders who dismiss the idea before listing its obstacles

## Green flags
- Idea involves hard, unpleasant, regulated or partner-heavy work that incumbents and other founders avoid
- Founder lived the pain (payments) and is willing to take the schlep
- Idea comes from "what do I wish someone would solve"

## Named examples
- Stripe: payments was painful for a decade; thousands of hackers knew it, yet built recipe sites. Needed deals with banks, fraud, regulation.
- Paul Graham in 1995: tried to convince himself he could start a company by just writing code
- Recipe sites / local events aggregators: low-stakes ideas chosen by schlep-blind founders

## Tensions
- Pushes toward ambitious, scary ideas, while most evaluations penalize regulatory or integration complexity.
- Ignorance is advertised as an advantage ("the most valuable antidote to schlep blindness is probably ignorance"), against advice to do all due diligence first.
- Younger founders are said to have an advantage through offsetting mistakes ("They don't know how much they can grow, but they also don't know how much they'll need to."), though "Ignorance can't solve everything".
