## One-line thesis
Do not try to think up startup ideas; live in the future at the edge of a fast-changing field, notice what is missing (preferably a problem you have), and build it, because made-up ideas sound plausible but have no urgent users.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Three-trait test**: The best ideas are wanted by the founders, buildable by them, and not seen as worth doing by others. Quote: "they're something the founders themselves want, that they themselves can build, and that few others realize are worth doing."
- **Problem must exist**: The most common failure is a nonexistent problem. Quote: "by far the most common mistake startups make is to solve problems no one has."
- **Sitcom idea detector**: A plausible-sounding idea that friends call "maybe I could see using" is a danger sign. Quote: "They say "Yeah, maybe I could see using something like that.""
- **Zero-sum of mild interest**: Summed mild interest is zero users. Quote: "Sum that reaction across the entire population, and you have zero users."
- **Plausible means assume bad**: Quote: "if all you know about a startup idea is that it sounds plausible, you have to assume it's bad."
- **Narrow and deep well**: Prefer a few who want it a lot over many who want it a little. Quote: "you can either build something a large number of people want a small amount, or something a small number of people want a large amount. Choose the latter."
- **Who wants this right now**: Name them. Quote: "Who wants this so much that they'll use it even when it's a crappy version one made by a two-person startup they've never heard of?"
- **Path out of the niche**: Depth is necessary but not sufficient; there must be a fast path out of the small market. Quote: "Facebook was a good idea because it started with a small market there was a fast path out of."
- **Paths out are hard to see**: Do not demand a proof of the big market. Quote: "The most important thing to understand about paths out of the initial idea is the meta-fact that these are hard to see."
- **Prepared mind**: Ideas come from a stimulus hitting a prepared mind. Quote: "it's generally the result of some external stimulus hitting a prepared mind."
- **Notice, don't think up**: Quote: "The verb you want to be using with respect to startup ideas is not "think up" but "notice.""
- **Organic ideas**: Ideas that grow from the founders' own experience. Quote: "At YC we call ideas that grow naturally out of the founders' own experiences "organic" startup ideas. The most successful startups almost all begin this way."
- **Live in the future**: Quote: "Live in the future, then build what's missing."
- **Turn off the big-company filter early**: Quote: "don't merely turn on the filter "What's missing?" Also turn off every other filter, particularly "Could this be a big company?""
- **Obvious to you**: Quote: "When you find the right sort of problem, you should probably be able to describe it as obvious, at least to you."
- **Chafing**: Quote: "Pay particular attention to things that chafe you."
- **Toy signal**: Dismissal as a toy is a positive sign. Quote: "At YC we're excited when we meet startups working on things that we could imagine know-it-alls on forums dismissing as toys."
- **Clash of domains**: Programmers entering a new field find problems because they do not take the status quo for granted. Quote: "you come into the new domain totally ignorant, you don't even know what the status quo is to take it for granted."
- **Research warning**: Quote: "the more a project has to count as research, the less likely it is to be something that could be turned into a startup."
- **Feeling late**: Quote: "Worrying that you're late is one of the signs of a good idea."
- **Competitors rarely kill**: Quote: "It's exceptionally rare for startups to be killed by competitors — so rare that you can almost discount the possibility."
- **Lock-in exception**: Quote: "unless you discover a competitor with the sort of lock-in that would prevent users from choosing you, don't discard the idea."
- **Beachhead quality**: Check who is in the beachhead. Quote: "if the beachhead consists of people doing something lots more people will be doing in the future, then it's probably big enough no matter how small it is."
- **Crowded market thesis**: Need a precise thesis about what incumbents overlook. Quote: "You don't need to worry about entering a "crowded market" so long as you have a thesis about what everyone else in it is overlooking."
- **Incumbents lacking courage of convictions**: Quote: "Best of all is when you can say that they didn't have the courage of their convictions"
- **Crowded market is a good sign**: Quote: "A crowded market is actually a good sign, because it means both that there's demand and that none of the existing solutions are good enough."
- **Schlep filter**: Quote: "Turning off the schlep filter is more important than turning off the unsexy filter, because the schlep filter is more likely to be an illusion."
- **Seek unsexy ideas**: Quote: "Make something unsexy that people will pay you for."
- **Would you use it yourself**: Quote: "Would you use this thing yourself, if you hadn't written it?"
- **Why doesn't someone make x**: Quote: "Why doesn't someone make x? If someone made x we'd buy it in a second."
- **Sell before building**: Quote: "tell them to go sell the product ASAP (before wasting time building it)."
- **Expertise judgment**: Search where you can judge idea quality. Quote: "you're giving yourself a Dunning-Kruger pass in that domain."
- **Replacement comes from the side**: Quote: "When one company or industry replaces another, it usually comes in from the side."
- **Ignored small market with disdain**: Quote: "It's particularly good if there's an admixture of disdain in the big players' attitude, because that often misleads them."
- **Ride a wave**: Quote: "the most successful startups generally ride some wave bigger than themselves"
- **Activation energy**: Quote: "the activation energy for enterprise software sold through traditional channels is very high, so you'd have to be a lot better to get users to switch."
- **Consultant mode for another's need**: Quote: "act like consultants — that they do what they'd do if they'd been retained to solve the problems of this one user."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Who wants this right now, so badly they would use a crappy version one? — only "lots of people could see using it".
- Would you use this yourself if you hadn't written it? — "no" or hesitation.
- How did you come to notice this problem? — brainstormed from a list of markets.
- What do the incumbents overlook, in one sentence more precise than "ours doesn't suck"? — no thesis.
- Where does the initial niche lead after it is won? — no path, or only a niche that cannot expand.
- Have you tried to sell it before building it? — never talked to a buyer.
- What schlep are you avoiding in this space? — nobody can name one, which suggests the easy idea space is crowded.
- Why you, why now, which wave are you riding? — no wave, no edge.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Idea has "sitcom" shape such as a social network or portal "for x" — "no one is going to visit 20 different communities regularly."
- Friends say "maybe" rather than "I need that".
- Founder cannot say they would use it themselves.
- Idea arrived by sitting down to "think of startup ideas".
- Feels like an inspiration while forcing ideas — "you're probably mistaken."
- Building in a domain where the founders cannot judge quality (database expert and a teen chat app).
- Idea is a pure research project.
- A competitor with real lock-in that prevents users from choosing you.
- Attached to a model of the world not tested with users (the art gallery lesson).

## Green flags
- Idea feels obvious to the founders; they chafe at the status quo.
- Dismissed as a toy by know-it-alls.
- Founders are the target users and can build version one tonight.
- A narrow group wants it urgently; a fast path out exists.
- Crowded market with a thesis on what incumbents overlook.
- Schlep-heavy or unsexy area that others shrink from.
- Incumbents disdain or ignore the market.
- Founders had a second life: they work on projects driven by curiosity.

## Named examples
- Microsoft (Altair Basic), Facebook (Harvard), Apple, Google, Yahoo: began as things founders built because there seemed a gap; well-shaped demand with a path out.
- Airbnb: initial idea was floor space at conventions; the expansion "forced itself upon them".
- Drew Houston, Dropbox: forgot a USB stick; earlier less promising SAT prep idea.
- Stripe: turned off the schlep filter on payments; users "desperately waiting".
- Viaweb: obvious to programmers that stores would be generated by software.
- Paul Graham's art gallery startup 1995: solved a problem no one has.
- Pet-owner social network: canonical sitcom idea.
- Hotmail: began as a tool to talk about the previous startup idea.
- E la Carte, Rajat Suri: became a waiter to learn restaurants.
- Wozniak and HP: HP declined partly out of disdain for a TV as monitor.
- Google vs earlier search engines: they shied from the radical implication that better results make users leave faster.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the "think of an idea, then validate" workflow; says to prepare the mind and notice.
- Says paths out of the niche are "hard to see", so do not require a TAM argument upfront, which differs from VC market-size filters.
- "Err on the side of doing things where you'll face competitors" contrasts with "avoid crowded markets".
- Contradicts "learn entrepreneurship" in college.
- Admits the unsexy filter is partly useful while the schlep filter is an illusion.
- Note 12 (both strategies, small-market-grows versus entering with a secret weapon) shows the two paths can blur.
