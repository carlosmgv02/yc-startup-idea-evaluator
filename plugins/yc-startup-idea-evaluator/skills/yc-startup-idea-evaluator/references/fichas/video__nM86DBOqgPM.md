## One-line thesis
Hard-tech milestones are financed by material proof objects (LOIs, then a built motor, then pre-orders), and the idea works when the incumbent technology scales badly for a specific segment (short regional flights); deliberately conventional design and risk-by-impact thinking cut cost.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Segment where incumbent tech is mismatched**: jet engines cost the same for short and long flights, pushing the industry toward bigger planes and longer routes. Quote: "that sort of segment of of of flying regionally is really badly served by the technology they have today"
- **Existing market, not new demand**: replacement of an old fleet. Quote: "There is a replacement market here that's not even driven by cost"
- **Operating economics as the real pitch**: advantage widened with a price shock. Quote: "gone from like being 33% better operating economics overall to 48% just because of this increase price in oil"
- **Domain work before the company**: the founder interviewed airlines while paid by a government before starting. Quote: "established the relationships uh with the airlines and the Nordics before I even like try to build something"
- **Sequence of proof**: LOIs without technology, then a physical motor, then pre-orders, then more capital. Quote: "every time you need more capital it needs to be something material"
- **Show the thing**: the customer "immediately got it" when shown a motor built at the size of the engine to replace. Quote: "brought them over showed show them the electric motor we built that was the size of the jet engines we tried to replace and they kind of immediately got it"
- **Inbound can come from a spam folder**: the first big airline customer. Quote: "United Airlines came through an inbound email through our info email"
- **Boring design on purpose**: hide novelty behind a conventional exterior. Quote: "I'd rather build something that looks very conventional but is kind of hiding its Superman cape under the hood"
- **In-house where suppliers multiply**: start in-house on the components that would need many suppliers. Quote: "it just scales with the number of technologies rather than the number of suppliers"
- **Minimize impact, not only probability**: SpaceX-style risk handling allows cheaper iteration. Quote: "how do we minimize impact uh to getting it wrong Then you can select a process that's a lot cheaper that's a lot easier to iterate on"
- **Hardest problem was not the headline technology**: reserves (diversion) forced a hybrid, adding about 20% of upfront cost. Quote: "The hardest problem had nothing to do with the motor or the battery"
- **Answer the substitutes**: pre-empt "why not flying taxis or hydrogen" with passenger count and infrastructure that already exists. Quote: "We're also operating from an infrastructure that is already there"
- **Appreciating asset framing**: the product improves after sale. Quote: "The plane will actually be better in 10 years than when you buy it"
- **Founder motivation test**: problem, not title. Quote: "I would never start a company based on the fact that I wanted to have a company or be a founder"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What material object can you show a customer in the next three months? — Only slides.
- Which segment does the incumbent technology serve badly, and by how much? — General claim of being better.
- What is the hardest problem that has nothing to do with your headline technology? — Not identified.
- Which components do you make in-house because they need many suppliers? — Everything outsourced, with long lead times.
- How do you minimize the impact of failure instead of its probability? — Waterfall certainty required before any test.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Raising on narrative with no physical proof between rounds.
- Futuristic design where conventional would do.
- Ignoring the reserve and edge-case constraints that dominate real operations.

## Green flags
- LOIs from several regional airlines before technology existed.
- Customer pull after seeing a physical prototype.
- Founder who spent years speaking to buyers before building.

## Named examples
- Heart Aerospace: 40-person team in Los Angeles, ES30 target of 125 miles electric and 500 miles hybrid, first flight of the demonstrator in August.
- United Airlines and SAS (and other Nordic airlines): LOIs and pre-orders.
- SpaceX "energy bar" risk philosophy and Tesla Model 3's single screen as a design analogy.

## Tensions
- Contradicts "launch fast with an MVP" for regulated hardware, replacing it with staged material milestones.
- Fuel and battery numbers (for example "$5" of electricity per flight, "$40" of fuel) are from the company video and are unverified.
