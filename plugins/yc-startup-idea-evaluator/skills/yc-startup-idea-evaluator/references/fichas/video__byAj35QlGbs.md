## One-line thesis
Pick a mission you love and can't drop, then cut the cost of iteration (software-ize engineering, vertically integrate) so a tiny team can do what used to take billions; "why now" is a weaker filter than founders think.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **"Why now" is overrated**: do not kill an idea just because it could have been built earlier and nobody did; check whether it is truly unsolved rather than assuming "someone would have done it". Quote: "the answer to why now can be because I started now"
- **Hidden-in-plain-sight problems**: crowds of founders chase only recently-possible ideas (AI, photo apps); the gap is in old, solvable, unbuilt things. Quote: "many great ideas hiding in plain sight that were actually solvable that could have been multi-billion or multi-t trillion dollar businesses a while ago but nobody's building them"
- **Founder-mission fit beats domain fit**: judge whether the founder loves the problem, not whether the resume matches. Passion can't be learned, knowledge can. Quote: "knowledge is changeable passion is not and there never has there been uh uh uh more reason to work on what you love"
- **Worst-day / best-day test**: ask whether the founder can survive near-death moments because the outcome matters to them. Quote: "as a startup founder you really have to optimize for two days the worst day and the best day"
- **Iteration cost is the hardware moat**: a hardware/deep-tech idea is more fundable if the team can lower the cost per iteration (digital design tools, in-house shop, own test assets). Quote: "the key thing is to make hardware development look more like software development to reduce the cost of iteration"
- **Product-market fit by analysis when you can't ship-to-learn**: where one build costs billions, PMF must be modeled digitally before building. Quote: "you can't get to product market fit on a supersonic jet by building it and seeing if anybody likes it"
- **Vertical integration to beat supplier latency**: if outside suppliers slow iteration, owning the step can be the advantage. Quote: "it's extremely difficult to iterate with outside suppliers particularly outside suppliers in aerospace"
- **Capital-intensity bridge via a spin-off product**: look for an early-revenue product from the same core tech that funds the long R&D. Quote: "we were able to take the engine that we were building for supersonic flight and sell it separately on the ground as a natural gas power turbine"
- **Regulation: context decides engage vs. ignore**: ignore regulators when there is an entrenched incumbent weaponizing them and customers can be your allies (Uber); engage early when safety-critical and no active enemy. Quote: "I don't think this is one-sizefits-all i think this is very contextual"
- **Regulation can be changed by demonstration**: do not treat a regulated industry as a blocker; a working demo changes the rules. Quote: "you can change the regulations and the way you do it is just by building the thing and communicating why it's okay"
- **Plan the first prototype to give multiple shots on goal**: avoid one giant first prototype that tests everything at once. Quote: "what we should have planned is something simpler and easier that that would give us multiple shots on goal"
- **Hire for caring and early-career hunger**: Quote: "the one thing I've never been able to teach somebody else is to care"
- **Expertise as liability**: do not require deep prior expertise; consult those who have done it but do not defer. Quote: "the moment you become an expert what you're steeped in is the past and then you're completely useless"
- **AI lowers custom-software cost, so users become tool builders**: bespoke software that fits an operation becomes affordable for small companies. Quote: "AI allows uh uh anybody who is a user to become a tool builder"
- **Physical-world AI gap**: manual industrial tasks without working AI are an open opportunity. Quote: "despite lots of pitches about AI for uh uh industrial automation we haven't yet seen much that works but I think that's going to come"
- **Don't start a company you will be trapped in**: Quote: "start a company but only if one only if it's a company you want to keep"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Why does this exist only if you build it, and why hasn't an incumbent or other startup done it? — Red flag: the only answer is a market-timing story that is not specific, or "someone must have tried".
- What is your cost and time per iteration, and how will you cut it? — Red flag: iteration needs outside parties and slots you do not control.
- How does the company fund a long R&D cycle? — Red flag: all revenue arrives after the full product ships.
- Who is the entrenched enemy and who is your ally in the regulator or customer base? — Red flag: you have not mapped who uses the rules against competitors.
- Is there a simpler first prototype that gives several shots on goal? — Red flag: the plan is a single all-or-nothing build.
- What would you do on the worst day (seven days of cash)? — Red flag: founder motivation depends on easy funding.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Founder picked the idea from resume fit and does not care about the product — per the source, produced a company he "didn't really give a shit about" and no product vision.
- Starting a company only to start one — "a good uh recipe for misery"; the founder gets trapped by debt to employees and investors.
- Ideas needing outside suppliers for every iteration with no plan to integrate.
- Assuming a regulated industry is closed rather than testing demonstration.

## Green flags
- Founder had a clear lifetime mission and "can't get it out of my head".
- Digital-first engineering (config-file airplane, simulation in minutes) enabling studies of what to build.
- Small team (50 people) with high output; build-in-public that creates tailwind.
- Spin-off product from core tech funds R&D; regulators engaged as part of the team (permit in 90 minutes).

## Named examples
- Boom Supersonic / XB1: first independently developed supersonic jet; executive order making supersonic flight legal came 115 days after breaking the sound barrier.
- Makeboom / Bladerunner [sic "Bladeunner"]: in-house design tools replacing spreadsheet engineering.
- Superpower: supersonic engine sold as ground natural-gas turbine to finance the airliner.
- Uber (Travis): ignore regulators, use customers as allies; contrasted with Boom engaging the FAA.
- Amazon early days: custom in-house software as an operating advantage.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the standard investor question "why now?": he argues it can be "because I started now".
- Contradicts "work on what you know": says passion beats domain knowledge.
- Contradicts "hardware needs deep experience": says the barriers are not that high, but also admits the company nearly died repeatedly and took 10+ years; he does not recommend following that path on funding.
