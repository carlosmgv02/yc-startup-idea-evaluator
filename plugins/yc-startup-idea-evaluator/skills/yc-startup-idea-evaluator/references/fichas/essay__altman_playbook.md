## One-line thesis
A startup needs a great idea (including market), team, product and execution, and the product part is non-negotiable: make something a small number of users love, because nothing else will save you.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Love over like**: prefer a product a few users love to one many like; growing users is easier than converting like into love. Quote: "It’s much better to first make a product a small number of users love than a product that a large number of users like."
- **Clear, concise answer**: founder must state what they build and why in a short answer; complexity signals muddled thinking or an invented problem. Quote: "complex ideas are almost always a sign of muddled thinking or a made up problem."
- **Excitement on first hearing**: the idea should excite at least some people the first time they hear it. Quote: "If the idea does not really excite at least some people the first time they hear it, that’s bad."
- **Who desperately needs it**: best case the founder is the target user, second best they understand the user extremely well. Quote: "In the best case, you yourself are the target user. In the second best case, you understand the target user extremely well."
- **Test by launching or selling**: consumer ideas, launch and watch (stated intent does not cut through clutter); enterprise ideas, get a letter of intent before writing code. Quote: "if you are an enterprise company, one of the first questions we’ll ask you is if you have a letter of intent from a customer saying they’ll buy what you’re building."
- **Unprompted word of mouth**: love means users tell friends without the company asking. Quote: "Usually this means they’re telling their friends to use the product without prompting from the company."
- **Monopoly / scale advantage**: look for businesses that get stronger with scale and are hard to copy. Quote: "we’re looking for businesses that get more powerful with scale and that are difficult to copy."
- **Market**: ask size today, growth rate, why big in ten years; favour early technological shifts big companies miss; large share of a small market is the best answer. Quote: "somewhat counterintuitively, the best answer is going after a large part of a small market."
- **New beats derivative**: 10x better is an acceptable definition of new; many copycats starting at once is a skeptical signal. Quote: "If there are ten other companies starting at the same time with the same plan, and it sounds a whole lot like something that already exists, we are skeptical."
- **Best ideas sound bad**: do not discount an idea because it sounds unattractive; secrecy is unnecessary. Quote: "The best ideas sound bad but are in fact good."
- **Idea first, startup second**: ideas forced after incorporating end up plausible but derivative; "no idea" teams all failed. Quote: "All of them failed."
- **Founder traits**: unstoppability, determination, resourcefulness beat experience and framework expertise. Quote: "The most important characteristics are ones like unstoppability, determination, formidability, and resourcefulness."
- **Responsiveness and communication**: hard-to-talk-to founders are bad; responsiveness indicates decisiveness. Quote: "Founders that are hard to talk to are almost always bad."
- **Team composition**: need a builder and someone who sells or talks to users (can be one person). Quote: "Tech startups need at least one founder who can build the company’s product or service, and at least one founder who is (or can become) good at sales and talking to users."
- **Cofounder history**: prefer a cofounder you know well over one met to start the company. Quote: "Cofounder breakups are one of the leading causes of death for early startups"
- **Product-failure diagnostic questions**: repeat use, fanatical users, bummed if gone, unprompted recommendations, 10 paying B2B customers. Quote: "If you’re a B2B company, do you have at least 10 paying customers?"
- **Skeptical of growth excuses**: lack of growth usually means the product is not good enough. Quote: "very often the real reason is that the product just isn’t good enough."
- **Vanity metrics**: signups without retention is the classic error. Quote: "The common mistake here is to focus on signups and ignore retention."
- **Unit economics later**: bad early economics OK only with a credible reason they work out later. Quote: "you have to have a good reason for why the unit economics are going to work out later."
- **LTV thresholds**: paid product under $500 LTV cannot afford sales (repay CAC in 3 months); over $500 net LTV can afford direct sales. Quote: "If you have a paid product with more than a $500 LTV (net to you) you generally can afford direct sales."
- **Competitors**: ignore until they beat you with a shipped product. Quote: "Do not worry about a competitor until they are beating you with a real, shipped product."
- **Investor-return lens**: must explain why you could be huge, not just a good small business. Quote: "Always explain why you could be a huge success."

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What are you building and why, in two sentences? — long or jargon-heavy answer, or a problem that sounds invented.
- Who desperately needs this? — "everyone", or a user the founder does not know well.
- How many users, how fast growing, and why not faster? — no explanation, or excuses other than product quality.
- Are users telling friends without being asked? Would they be truly bummed if you disappeared? — no to both.
- (Enterprise) Do you have a letter of intent from a customer? Do you have 10 paying customers? — none, and no plan to get them.
- How will this company one day be a monopoly? — nothing gets stronger with scale or harder to copy.
- Why will this market be big in ten years? — no technological shift or growth reason.
- Why do you want to start this particular company? — no sustaining mission.
- If revenue is zero, why? — no real reason.
- What is the minimum kernel to build first to test the hypothesis? — insists on building the full product first.
- If unit economics are bad today, why will they work later? — no reason offered.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Idea is derivative and many identical teams are starting at once — "we are skeptical".
- Founders with no idea who incorporate first — the experiment of funding such teams: "All of them failed."
- Founders met only to start the company — cofounder breakup risk.
- Founder is slow-moving — "I have never, not once, seen a slow-moving founder be really successful."
- Excuses for lack of growth that are not the product — real reason is usually product quality.
- Trying to grow via deals with other companies or a big press launch — "effectively never work".
- Founder deceiving themselves that users love the product — "you will still fail".
- Early-stage founder with own publicist or personal-brand focus — "they almost always fail".
- Betting on a hired experienced manager to do the CEO's work — "a graveyard for failed companies".
- Ad-supported plan that relies on buying users.
- Raising when the company is not in shape — burns reputation.

## Green flags
- Founder is the target user.
- Users recruited one at a time who then tell their friends unprompted.
- Weekly product improvement cycle driven by watching users.
- Fast growth even if everything is unoptimized.
- Enterprise founder with a customer letter of intent before code.
- Founder who is both rigid on mission and flexible on everything else.
- Founder who gets close to users: does sales and support personally.
- Reaching ramen profitability early.

## Named examples
- Pinterest (Ben Silbermann): approached strangers in coffee shops to recruit early users manually.
- Airbnb: posted a forward-looking growth graph everywhere; cited for mission and values.
- Facebook: created a prestigious growth group when growth slowed (per Zuckerberg).
- Friendster: died of technical debt, the rare case of scaling problems killing a startup.
- Ford: "people would have asked Ford for faster horses", the limit of asking users.

## Tensions
- "Do things that don't scale" is capped by the need to eventually make money.
- Talking to users is the default remedy but "doesn't work in every case" (faster horses).
- Persistence vs obstinacy is called "a hard judgment call".
- Speed vs obsession with quality: "very hard to be both" yet the tell of a great founder.
- Ignore competitors 99% of the time, yet look for businesses hard to copy.
- Pivots are flagged as dangerous (derivative ideas), while the idea should "evolve as you get feedback".
