## One-line thesis
Regulation decides whether some technologies can reach market at all; founders should know if their product is "born free" (no rules yet) or "born in captivity" (needs a government approval), because that sets the real obstacle and timeline.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Born free vs born in captivity**: classify the product by whether it needs government approval to be sold. Quote: "There's there's there's technologies that are either born free or born in captivity"
- **Approval-gated markets**: if the product cannot be commercialized without a waiver or rule change, the regulatory path is the critical path. Quote: "born in captivity technologies are technologies where you're building something but you can't commercialize it or you can't take it out to market unless you get some sort of government approval"
- **Rule design can unlock a category**: some ideas wait on a specific rule (example: supersonic flight). Quote: "he can't get his his supersonic plane to fly unless the rules are set such that there is a noise limit instead of a speed limit"
- **Fragmented regulation favors incumbents**: a patchwork of rules is a cost large companies absorb and startups cannot. Quote: "the big tech guys they can deal with that I mean Google can har can hire an army of lawyers"
- **Regulation lags fast technology**: hard thresholds written early tend to age badly (EU AI Act written before LLMs). Quote: "you don't want to set very uh firm red lines in the sand because they ultimately don't work"
- **Government as unlocker**: policy actions can open markets (drones, autonomous vehicles). Quote: "there was this government angle where the government's policy actions could be ones that could actually unlock technology"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- Can you legally sell this today, or do you need an approval, waiver or rule change? — red flag: founder has not checked.
- If approval is needed, who grants it, how long does it take, and what do you do meanwhile? — red flag: no plan for the regulatory timeline.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Business depends on a rule change with no path or timeline.

## Green flags
- Founder understands the specific rule that gates the market and has a plan for it.

## Named examples
- Commercial drones: need an FAA waiver to operate.
- Boom Supersonic: needs a noise-based rule instead of a speed limit over land.
- EU AI Act: finalized before ChatGPT, an example of rules written too early.

## Tensions
- Speaker is a government official describing his administration's policy; statements about future deregulation are policy positions, not evidence.
