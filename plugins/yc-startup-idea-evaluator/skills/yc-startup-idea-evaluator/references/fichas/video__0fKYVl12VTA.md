## One-line thesis
Founders can and must sell their own first enterprise deals: qualify hard on problem, budget and authority, run the whole funnel (prospect, qualify, demo, price, close, implement) as a listening exercise, and own implementation until the customer uses the product habitually.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Founder-led sales pre-PMF**: if the founders cannot sell it themselves, a hired salesperson will not fix that; pre-PMF sales is entrepreneurial and needs a tight loop with the builders. Quote: "if you aren't able to sell your product yourself at first chances are you're not going to be able to hire somebody else to do it for you"
- **Written sales hypothesis**: before prospecting, state "customer X has problem Y and our product will help them solve it"; if you cannot name who to talk to, the hypothesis is too vague. Quote: "customer X has problem Y and our product will help them solve it"
- **Talk to buyers, not the easy-to-reach**: check that the people taking your calls match the real buyer profile; easy-to-talk-to people select for bad customers. Quote: "it selects for the people who are easiest to talk to not the people who will be be great customers"
- **Fake progress from bad customers**: feedback from non-buyers feels like traction but is useless or harmful. Quote: "talking to bad customers gives you the illusion that you're making progress when you're not"
- **Do not sell big-company problems to startups**: if the problem only exists once a company is large (HRIS example), selling to startups is wasted time. Quote: "if your product solves a problem that companies only have when they get big like for example an HR information system then trying to sell it to startups is a waste of time"
- **Bottom-up vs top-down fit**: if adoption needs many teams to coordinate (hospital example: CIO, software team, doctors, ops), talking to individual contributors is useless; you need the CFO/CIO. Quote: "trying to go bottom up with a product that needs to be adopted top down"
- **Sell fast, but only to real buyers**: the "sell to whoever buys quickly" meme does not mean selling to non-needers. Quote: "you need to find people who have the problem you're solving and the budget and decision-making authority to buy your product"
- **First call = qualification, not pitch**: goals are (1) problem, budget, authority, (2) book the demo. Good: mostly questions and listening. Quote: "great salespeople spend most of their time listening because that's the best way to understand someone's problem"
- **Question set**: why take the call, how long, how bad, who else affected, how quantified, why unsolved, budget, buying process, who decides. A prospect who has no problem or does not care enough is a successful disqualification. Quote: "why haven't you solved it already what's your budget for solving it how does your organization buy software who makes the buying decision"
- **Demo as story, not feature tour**: recap the user and her problem, show one flow with magic moments, personalize with their logo, site, customers. Quote: "your job in a demo is not to show off your product it's to convince your audience that you can help them solve their problem"
- **Pricing is an experiment**: no formula; use each conversation to test a price and read the reaction; optimize for learning, not unit economics. Quote: "think of each pricing conversation you have as an opportunity to run an experiment"
- **Quote high**: undercharging or free-for-feedback is the commonest mistake; real demand survives a high quote. Quote: "when a customer really wants your product it's hard to scare them away by quoting a price that's too high"
- **Price as a demand test**: a sale at a higher-than-competition price is evidence of real need. Quote: "higher prices can help you figure out whether customers actually need your product"
- **Closing is a process, map it early**: ask upfront how they buy and who signs; start security questionnaires in parallel; keep legal docs simple (Common Paper templates). Quote: "the biggest mistake that I see Founders make at this stage is getting surprised and discovering that what they thought was a done deal is in fact not done at all"
- **Champion as ally**: stay in constant contact and ask the champion first when stuck, since they cannot solve their problem until procurement passes. Quote: "your prospect who at this point has become your Champion is your biggest Ally"
- **Implementation is the vendor's job**: customers buy a solution, not a product; plan implementation before signing and refuse to sign if it cannot be planned. Quote: "the single biggest mistake that Founders make is thinking that implementation is the customer's job"
- **Sales ends at habitual use**: the funnel closes when usage is habitual, not at signature. Quote: "your sales funnel only really ends when your customer is using your product habitually"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is your sales hypothesis in one sentence (customer X, problem Y)? — Cannot name a specific buyer role and company type.
- Who do you actually have budget and decision authority with today? — Only users, interns or other startups who "like it".
- What price have you quoted and how did they react? — Never quoted, or quoted free/cheap out of fear.
- Who must sign off (security, legal, procurement, IT) and how long did it take last time? — No map of the buying process.
- Who owns implementation and what is the plan before signature? — "The customer will integrate it themselves".
- Of your last 10 prospects, how many had the problem, the budget and the authority? — Counting calls taken instead of qualified buyers.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Selling enterprise-only software to startups because they answer emails — they lack the problem and the feedback misleads.
- Pitching in the first call without questions — misreads sales as adversarial.
- Giving the product away free for feedback — hides whether anyone will pay.
- Signed deals that nobody uses (Optimizely renewal story) — churn at renewal; implementation was neglected.
- A "done deal" with no known procurement path.

## Green flags
- Founders (even technical) personally closing first customers.
- Prospects who negotiate price down instead of walking.
- Inbound demand from content, demos, forums or conferences.
- Customer champion actively helping through procurement.
- Shared implementation roadmap with owners and regular check-ins before the contract.

## Named examples
- Optimizely (Winter 2010): hypothesis of marketers wanting no-code A/B tests; demo on the prospect's own site; six-figure deals with zero usage taught implementation ownership. Quote of $10,000/month asked, $2,000 accepted.
- Stripe (Collison brothers): charged more than competition early as proof of need.
- Common Paper: YC company with open-source contract templates.
- Peter Kazanjy, Founding Sales: recommended free book.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the "talk to anyone who will take your call" instinct and the "sell to whoever buys fastest" reading of YC advice.
- Says you do not need a business co-founder, while also saying hiring a salesperson early will not work.
- Free design partnerships/feedback are discouraged; compare with Blomfield (paid commitment) in the same corpus.
