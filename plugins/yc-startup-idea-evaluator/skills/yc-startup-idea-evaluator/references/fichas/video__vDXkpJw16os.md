## One-line thesis
The initial idea does not have to be great; start from a problem you personally know, ask why you are uniquely placed, ship a throwaway MVP in days or weeks, and test with hand-picked early users whether it solves the problem for any group.

## Evaluation heuristics
- **Idea need not be great**: Quote: "there's a common misconception that your idea has to be great in order start a company"
- **Start with a problem, not an idea**: Quote: "starting with ideas is tricky because immediately people want to grade your idea"
- **Personal relationship with the problem**: Check whether the founder, friends, family, work or community have it. Quote: "do I have any relationship with this problem personally is this a problem that I have is a problem that my friends have"
- **Personal connection reason 1**: Judging the solution. Quote: "you can tell whether this solution is even within the ballpark of solving the problem"
- **Personal connection reason 2**: Persistence. Quote: "when you're feeling discouraged and the solutions that you're building are working you still have some personal connection to the problem"
- **Problem book**: Quote: "they'd have a lot more success if they kept little problem books"
- **Brainstorm with potential cofounders**: Quote: "a lot of recruiting co-founders is about having really good brainstorming sessions"
- **Uniquely qualified**: Not in the resume sense; an angle others do not understand. Quote: "is there some angle on the problem that you understand that you don't think others understand"
- **Study prior attempts**: Quote: "just so you can get a basic understanding of what other people thought their unique insight was"
- **Do not fall in love with the MVP**: Quote: "your MVP is most likely not going to solve the problem or is going to solve the problem very poorly"
- **Love the problem**: Quote: "be in love with your problem be in love with the cause tomorrow" [sic]
- **Release quickly**: Quote: "an MVP can be built and released in days or weeks"
- **Hand-picked early users**: Quote: "your initial users should almost be hand-picked"
- **Customers must care enough**: Do not solve for someone unwilling to try a startup or for whom the problem is unimportant. Quote: "the problem you're trying to solve is not a really important problem in their life"
- **Goal of MVP**: Not usage counts but whether it solves the problem for any group. Quote: "your goal here with your MVP is not to see how many people want to use your product"
- **Filter users**: Quote: "the best startups actually very heavily filter the people who are able to use the initial product"

## Questions a YC partner would ask (derived from this source)
- What is your personal relationship with this problem? — none, an observed market only.
- Why are you uniquely qualified: what angle do others miss? — only years of experience, no insight.
- What did earlier attempts think their insight was, and how is yours different? — never looked.
- What is the first MVP and how fast will it be in front of users? — months of building.
- Which group is it solving the problem for, and who did you choose as first users? — "anyone".
- Are those users people for whom the problem is really important and who will try a startup? — casual interest only.

## Red flags / kill signals
- Falling in love with the product rather than the problem.
- Waiting to release; perfecting before learning.
- Measuring success by how many people want to use the MVP rather than whether it solves the problem.
- Early customers not willing to try a startup, or for whom the problem is minor.
- Starting from a graded idea instead of a problem (idea books).

## Green flags
- Personal or close-network connection to the problem.
- A specific angle or perspective others do not have.
- MVP shipped in days or weeks and treated as a learning step.
- Very selective first customers.
- Founders brainstorm problems together and work well as a team.

## Named examples
- Justin.tv to Twitch: original idea was an online reality TV show; became a live video game site, sold to Amazon "for almost a billion dollars"; the initial idea was not great and still worked.

## Tensions
- Contradicts the view that you need a great idea to start.
- Nuances "build for everyone" by asking for heavily filtered, hand-picked first users.
- Success of the MVP is "does it solve the problem for any group", not a usage or sign-up count, against growth-metric-first thinking.
- "Be in love with the problem, not the product" nuances founder-attachment advice.
